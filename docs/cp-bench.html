Method

Why water is the whole game

Carnivory is an adaptation to soils so poor in nutrients that catching animals is a cheaper way to get nitrogen than absorbing it through roots. The corollary is that these plants have no mechanism for dealing with dissolved minerals arriving at the root, because in their habitat there are none. Tap water at 300 ppm is not slightly worse than rainwater; it is a different environment.

The published figure this page uses is TDS best below 160 ppm (California Carnivores). Many growers work to a much stricter target of around 50 ppm, and reverse-osmosis output is typically well under that. The 50 ppm figure is a convention rather than a sourced threshold, and this page labels it as one.

A TDS meter measures total dissolved solids by electrical conductivity. It does not tell you which solids, and that matters: 100 ppm of calcium bicarbonate is a different problem from 100 ppm of sodium chloride. For growing purposes the total is a good enough proxy, but it is a proxy.

The tray arithmetic

This is the calculation most growers never make. A pot standing in a tray loses water almost entirely to evaporation and transpiration. Both processes leave the dissolved solids behind. So if you top the tray up n times with water at C ppm without ever flushing, the solids in the pot accumulate:

solids after n top-ups ≈ n × C ppm × (tray volume)

Ten top-ups with 50 ppm water puts as much dissolved solid into the pot as one watering at 500 ppm would. That is why growers who use good water and still lose plants are usually growers who never flush.

Read the number as an upper bound. The estimate assumes every dissolved solid stays in the pot and stays soluble. In reality some precipitates out of solution, some is taken up, some is bound to the peat, and rain flushes outdoor pots for you. It is a ceiling on accumulation, and its job is to tell you roughly how often to flush — not to predict a concentration you could measure.

Flushing means pouring clean water through the top of the pot until it runs freely from the bottom, several times, and emptying the tray. Doing that periodically resets the arithmetic.

Why the dormancy numbers are not in this app

The fact that a genus contains temperate species requiring a winter dormancy is well established, and this page ships it. The numbers — how cold, for how many weeks, at what photoperiod — are not here, and their absence is deliberate.

Researching this before building the page turned up no citable primary source for per-genus dormancy temperatures or durations. Figures circulate widely in the hobby, they disagree with each other, and they are usually stated without provenance. Under the honesty rule this kit works to (ADR-031, gate 3), a number that is regionally variable, unsourceable and consequential does not get shipped in software.

So the Season tab asks for your target — from your society, your climate, your supplier, or your own successful previous winters — records it alongside the data, and tracks against it. That is worse for a beginner who wanted to be told, and better for anyone who later has to explain why they did what they did.

Media

The preset mixes are hobby conventions: widely used, widely agreed, and not standards. They are a starting point to adapt to your climate, your pots and your watering habits — a mix that works in a humid greenhouse will dry too fast on a windowsill.

Two things are not conventions and matter more than the ratio. Every component must be nutrient-free — no fertiliser, no wetting agents, no lime. And sand must be silica: play sand and builder's sand are frequently calcareous, which raises pH and releases calcium, and that will kill the plant slowly enough that you blame the light.

Feeding

Plants in good light with reasonable prey access generally do not need feeding. Where growers do feed, one published protocol is a very dilute foliar feed — ¼ teaspoon of MaxSea per gallon, monthly, on the leaves only (California Carnivores). That is one vendor's protocol, not a standard, and it is genus-dependent: a regime that suits Nepenthes can burn a Dionaea. Root fertilising is how most people kill temperate species.

Where plants come from

Carnivorous plants are among the most heavily poached plant groups there are, and habitat loss has done the rest. Several taxa are legally protected: Sarracenia oreophila and S. alabamensis are US federally listed, Dionaea poaching is a felony in North Carolina, and Nepenthes are on CITES with several species on Appendix I.

The practical rule is simple and it costs nothing: buy from nurseries that propagate their own stock, and keep the provenance in the record. This page has a source field on every plant for exactly that reason. Divisions and seed-grown plants from known cultivated parents are better material anyway — they are adapted to cultivation rather than to a bog.

If you have a wild-collected plant of unknown legality, that is a fact worth recording honestly rather than losing.

What this page does not do

It gives no light, humidity or temperature recommendations by species, for the same reason it gives no dormancy numbers. It does not identify plants or verify hybrid parentage — a cross record is a record of what you did, and a hybrid's identity rests on that record being honest. And it does not track pesticide applications, which are genus-specific and where the wrong product kills the plant faster than the pest.
