Enter a circumference instead if that's what your tape gives: DBH = C ÷ π. Measure at the breast height you set on the Plot tab — 1.37 m is the North American convention, 1.30 m is used through most of the world — on the uphill side; for a fork below breast height, record each stem separately and say so in the note.

Height from two angles

The standard clinometer method. Works on slopes, which the single-angle shortcut does not.

Sign convention: looking up is positive, looking down is negative. Standing below the tree you'll read a negative base angle; standing above it, positive — and then the tree's height is the difference, which is why both readings matter. Distance must be horizontal, not slope distance: on a 20% slope, slope distance overestimates by about 2%. Leaning stems break the method entirely — sight from a direction perpendicular to the lean, or measure the stem length and note it.

Photo notes

Shoot the character, not the tree: bark, a cone, the underside of a spray, the needle attachment. Then write what you saw while you are still standing in front of it.

Add photos — camera or gallery, or drop files here.

How the photos are handled, plainly: they are held in this page for the session and never uploaded anywhere. Closing the tab discards them — the originals stay in your camera roll, which is where they belong. What the export carries is the filename, so your notes and your photos match up later. Interactions

What is doing what to what. A stand is a set of relationships and this is where you record them — observed, inferred, or read.

Recording the evidence class and confidence separately is the point. A nutcracker you watched cache a seed and a mutualism you know from a paper are both worth having on the sheet, and they are not the same observation. Keep them distinguishable and your own data stays honest when someone else reads it.

Method notes

Every formula this page uses, its source, and where it breaks. Print this with the sheet and the numbers are defensible.

What the entry layer records — and what it does not

The controls on this page are the shared Field Entry Kit, sized so a gloved thumb on a wet tablet hits them. Two of them changed what the sheet stores, and the change is deliberate.

Aspect is eight compass points, not a free 0–360 field. A hand compass in the field, read while standing on a slope, does not give you a degree. Recording 227° when you read "southwest" is a precision you did not measure. The eight points fold to the McCune & Keon heat-load axis exactly as the continuous value would — folded aspect = |180 − |aspect − 225||, 0° at the cool northeast end and 180° at the hot southwest — and the fold is what the number is for. If you are working from a DEM rather than a compass, put the degree in the notes.

Cover moves in 5% steps. Trained observers do not agree with each other about cover, and the disagreement is bigger than people expect. Morrison's 2016 review of observer error in vegetation surveys found coefficients of variation of roughly 25–50% between observers even for abundant species above 50% cover, and that where observers used cover classes, something like a third to a half of estimates landed one class apart. Note the shape of that: it is a proportional error, not a flat number of percentage points, so it is worst in relative terms on the sparse species. Estimates cluster on multiples of five in any case, so a control offering 1% increments invites a precision the method does not have. Record the observer — between-observer variance is routinely larger than the effect you are looking for, which is why a re-survey by a different person is not a clean comparison unless you say so.

Blank still means blank. Aspect, slope position, texture, moisture and every cover field start unrecorded and stay out of the export until you touch them. Aspect 0° is north, and this sheet will not enter it on your behalf.

One plot is an anecdote. Every per-hectare number on this page is a point estimate from a single sample with no confidence interval attached. That is a property of your design, not of the arithmetic. Three plots give you a mean and a spread; ten give you something you can defend in a methods section. The page will happily compute a precise-looking BA/ha from four stems — precision is not accuracy, and the decimals are not evidence.

On the identification characters

The key here is a field aid, not a flora. It is built on the characters that hold up in bad light with cold hands — silhouette, needle attachment, bark, the one cone character that settles it. It will not resolve hybrids, and several of the hard pairs in this region (Abies concolor × magnifica around Tahoe, Pinus edulis × monophylla in western Utah, whitebark × limber pine) genuinely do intergrade. Where the key says two species are hard to separate, believe it, record both possibilities, take a voucher, and settle it with a regional flora.

Nomenclature follows common current usage; it moves. Juniperus grandis was split from J. occidentalis; Callitropsis / Xanthocyparis / Cupressus nootkatensis remains unsettled. Record what you saw and which authority you used.
