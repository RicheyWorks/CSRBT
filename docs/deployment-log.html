Where the clock will be when you come back

Open Acoustic Devices state that an AudioMoth's time is accurate to ±10 ms when set, and that the clock then drifts up to about one second per day. Their GPS-synchronised firmware reaches at least 20 µs between units. Those three numbers decide whether your array can localise anything at all. All three are from their two support pages, linked under Sources below.

Setup photographs

The one frame every camera-trap deployment needs and almost none has: the view the camera sees, taken from behind it, with a scale in it. A year later it is the only way to say whether the lens was blocked, how far the trail was, and what the vegetation looked like before it grew. Shoot the mount, the settings screen, and the walk-test too if you ran one.

The sheet carries the reference, not the image. The frames stay in this tab and are never uploaded. The field sheet gets the filename, a CRC-32 and when the file was taken — enough to match a deployment to its setup shot on the card. The CRC is an integrity check, not a cryptographic one.

Method

What this page refuses

It will not give you decibels

An AudioMoth’s gain settings are named low/medium/high, not calibrated. Open Acoustic Devices are explicit that getting sound pressure level out of one means calibrating the whole recording chain — microphone, case, and recording settings — against a calibrated meter, not the microphone alone. A case change alone moves the answer. So this page records your gain setting and refuses to convert anything to dB SPL. Relative amplitude between recordings made on one unit at one gain is a real measurement. An absolute level is not, and the page will not print one.

It will not call a converted RGB camera an NDVI sensor

NDVI is (NIR − Red) / (NIR + Red), and a filter-converted consumer camera does not have those two bands cleanly separated — it has three broad, overlapping channels and a filter in front. DroneDeploy’s own guidance is that the only appropriate index for a standard RGB camera is VARI, a visible-band index, and that NDVI needs a sensor with real red and near-infrared bands. This page names the index after the sensor you actually flew.

It will not let an uncalibrated flight be compared to another one

Pix4D put it plainly: “Radiometric correction is required to be able to compare multispectral imagery taken at different points in time under different weather conditions, and to compute reliable index values.” Without it, the pixel values carry the shutter speed, the ISO, the sun angle and the cloud — all of which change between flights and none of which are the plant. A reflectance panel corrects to reflectance; a downwelling light sensor tracks illumination changes during the flight, which a panel read once on the ground cannot. This page states which of the two you had and refuses to describe a flight with neither as comparable to anything.

It will not print a radiation-shield error figure

A temperature logger in direct sun reads its own casing, not the air, and the error is large. It is also specific to the shield, the wind speed and the sun angle, and the study this page would have cited — which found a gill-type multi-plate shelter gave the best protection of the configurations tested — is behind a paywall this page cannot read. So the finding ships, the number does not, and the page asks you which shield you used and records the answer. A plausible number with no source behind it is the one thing this kit will not hand you.

Clock drift, which is arithmetic

Sound travels about 343 m/s in air at 20 °C (the usual approximation is 331.3 + 0.606 T with T in °C, so 331.3 + 0.606 × 20 = 343.4). A recorder whose clock has drifted by one second is, for the purposes of time-of-arrival localisation, wrong about where it is by 343 metres. Over a fortnight at the stated drift rate that is several kilometres. This is why arrays need GPS synchronisation and why a single unit’s file timestamps need correcting before they are joined to anything else — and the page does the multiplication so the number is in front of you before you deploy, not after.
