The four sampling rules

The vocabulary here follows Altmann (1974), which is still the paper the field argues from.

rulewhat you dowhat it gives you — and does not
ad libitumWrite down whatever you happen to notice. Biased toward whatever is conspicuous, close, or performed by the boldest animal. Legitimate for generating hypotheses and for building a first ethogram. Never a source of rates or budgets.
focal animalOne named subject for a fixed period; record everything it does. The workhorse. Gives true durations and true frequencies for that individual. Costs you the rest of the group.
scanAt fixed instants, record what each visible subject is doing. Group-wide time budgets cheaply. Rare and brief behaviours are almost never caught, so it is poor for events.
behaviourWatch the whole group; record every occurrence of one or two chosen behaviours. The right tool for rare, conspicuous acts — fights, copulations, provisioning. Says nothing about anything you did not choose.

The three recording rules

ruleestimatesthe catch
continuousTrue duration and true frequency. Expensive, and only honest if you can genuinely keep up. A continuous record you fell behind on is worse than a point sample.
instantaneous (point)Proportion of samples in a state → unbiased estimate of proportion of time. Unbiased for states only. It cannot estimate frequency, and it will miss behaviours shorter than the interval entirely.
one-zeroWhether a behaviour occurred at all in each interval. Biased for both. It overestimates duration — a one-second act scores a whole interval — and underestimates frequency, because five occurrences score the same as one. See below.
On one-zero. Altmann's critique is that a one-zero score is not an unbiased estimator of duration, of frequency, or of anything else with a name; the quantity it estimates has no biological meaning of its own. It survives because it is easy and because the numbers correlate with duration well enough to look useful. This page will let you use it, will label every figure it produces as one-zero, and will not convert those figures into a time budget or a rate. If you report them, report them as what they are: the proportion of intervals containing the behaviour.

Time budget

Under continuous recording the budget is Σ duration in state / total observed time. Under instantaneous it is points in state / total points, with a binomial standard error SE = √(p(1−p)/n) — the same estimator a line-point intercept uses on vegetation, for the same reason.

Observed time is not elapsed time. Any period the subject was out of sight is excluded from the denominator, which is why out of sight is a first-class state here rather than a gap in the record. A budget computed over elapsed time silently attributes the animal's invisible minutes to whatever it was doing when it vanished.

Bout length is the mean duration of an uninterrupted spell in a state. It is meaningful only under continuous recording, and it is sensitive to how you defined the state's end — a definition that ends a bout at every head-turn produces many short bouts and says nothing about the animal.

Transitions

The matrix counts how often state i was followed by state j, then divides each row by its total to give transition probabilities. Two honest limits. First, this is a first-order Markov summary: it assumes where the animal goes next depends only on where it is now, which is usually false — what it was doing two states ago, and for how long, generally matter. Second, the diagonal is structurally empty, because states here are mutually exclusive and a state cannot follow itself; that is a property of the coding scheme, not a finding about the animal. Rows built on a handful of transitions are noise — the page marks those.

Reliability

Cohen's κ = (po − pe) / (1 − pe), where po is the proportion of samples the two observers scored identically and pe is the agreement expected if each observer had assigned categories at random with their own marginal frequencies. κ = 1 is perfect; κ = 0 is chance; negative κ is worse than chance.

The bands this page prints — slight, fair, moderate, substantial, almost perfect — are the Landis & Koch (1977) convention. They are a widely used rule of thumb and not a statistical result: the authors said as much when they proposed them. Report κ itself and the number of samples, not the adjective.

κ is also depressed when one category dominates, because pe rises — two observers who agree on 95% of samples can still score a low κ if 95% of samples are “resting”. That is the prevalence problem, and it is a reason to read κ beside the raw agreement rather than instead of it. Both are printed.

Pseudoreplication, and the thing that invalidates most student data

If you record forty scans of one animal, your sample size for a question about the species is one, not forty. Repeated measures on the same individual are not independent, and treating them as independent is the commonest fatal error in behavioural coursework. The fix is to make the individual the unit of analysis: compute each subject's budget, then analyse across subjects. This page reports per-subject figures for exactly that reason, and it will tell you when everything came from one animal.

If you genuinely cannot tell individuals apart, say so in the write-up and treat the group as the unit. That is a real limitation, and stating it is worth more than a p-value built on a sample size you did not have.
