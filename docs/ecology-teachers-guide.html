The Teacher's Guide

Seven labs, five field apps, and one honest idea about how science actually works. This is the document that tells you how to run them with thirty students, six tablets, and a building that only lets you outside twice a term.

For the teacher deciding on a Tuesday whether to use this on Monday.

The ninety-second answer

Do I need to install anything? No. Every page is a single file that opens in a browser — phone, tablet, Chromebook, the ancient desktop in the back. No accounts, no logins, no student data leaves the device.
Does it need internet in the field? Only to load the page the first time. After that a tablet in airplane mode in the middle of a field works fine. Nothing is uploaded; nothing needs a signal.
What does it cost? Nothing. MIT-licensed. The most expensive lab needs a bag of dried beans and a coat-hanger bent into a square.
Can I use just one lab? Yes, and you probably should. Start with the one-period lab — it needs no equipment and no fieldwork, and it tells you in fifty minutes whether the rest of this is for you.
Do students need to be good at math? No. The tools compute Shannon's H′ and a χ² for them and say the result in a sentence. The teaching happens in the argument about what the number means — which is the part worth class time anyway.
What if it rains? Three of the seven labs need no fieldwork at all, and one of them is a game about losing days to rain. There's a plan.

One

Three ways in

Pick by how much room you have, not by how much you like the idea.

One periodLab 7, Food Webs. No equipment, no fieldwork, no prep beyond opening a page. Students build a web by tapping, then break it and watch what starves. If a single lesson is going to convince you, it's this one.
One unitThree labs that share a spine: Biodiversity (3) → Mark–Recapture (4) → Food Webs (7). Two weeks. The through-line is you cannot count everything, so how do you know anything?
A termAll seven, in the sequence below, with the field apps carrying data collection and the workbench carrying the analysis. Twelve weeks with room to breathe.

If you only ever run one thing, run the food-web knockout. Students name eight organisms from a habitat they know, draw the feeding arrows, then long-press a species and watch the cascade. It stages Robert Paine's 1966 sea-star removal (Paine 1966, 1969) — the experiment that gave us the word keystone — in a form that fits between a bell and a bell. Students who have argued about which removal will hurt most, and then been wrong, do not forget what a keystone species is.

Read the "optional" column carefully. Labs 1 and 4 are marked optional rather than no because they are better outside but they genuinely work inside. Lab 1 runs on a five-minute video of a bird feeder. On standards. The labs map cleanly onto the ecosystem, heredity, and evolution strands of most frameworks — NGSS's life-science lines on ecosystem dynamics, inheritance, and natural selection; AP Biology's heredity and ecology units; IB Biology's ecology and genetics topics. I've deliberately not printed specific standard codes, because they get renumbered and a guide with stale codes is worse than one with none. Map them against your own framework once; it takes ten minutes and it'll be right.

Three

A twelve-week sequence

The order isn't the order of the manual. It's built so each lab needs a skill the one before it taught, and so the two fieldwork days land where the weather is likeliest to hold.

Week 1
Food Webs — the hook

Lab 7. No equipment. Students build a web of a habitat they already know and break it. Ends with the first argument of the term: was the species you removed important, or just common? You are teaching the distinction on day one and coming back to it all term.

Week 2
Sampling, before any data exists

Field Season, the game. Every student gets the same hidden meadow (same season number = same world), twelve field days, and weather that takes some of them away. They file a report and it gets graded against the truth. They will lose. Then you compare strategies across the room and the phrase "sampling effort" means something.

Week 3
Behavioral observation

Lab 1, indoors on video first. Build the ethogram as a class — this is where students learn that a definition is an instrument. Then the Field Notebook does the scan timer and the tallies.

Week 4
Behavior, outside — first field day

Same lab, real animals, groups of three. The comparison that makes the lesson: their video budget versus their live budget. Which behaviors did scan sampling miss?

Weeks 5–6
Biodiversity & community — second field day

Lab 3 across two weeks: quadrats and counts in week 5, analysis and the two-site comparison in week 6. This is the term's biggest field day; put it where your weather is best. The payoff is watching Sørensen and Bray–Curtis disagree about the same two sites, and having to say why.

Week 7
Mark & recapture

Lab 4 with beans, indoors. Because you know the true number, every group's error is visible and comparable. Then break an assumption on purpose — add unmarked beans between catches — and watch the estimate move in the direction the math demands.

Week 8
Island biogeography

Lab 6, browser only. Halve the island, double it, watch equilibrium richness follow the curves. The moment worth waiting for: richness holds steady while the residents keep changing. "Full" and "unchanging" turn out to be different claims.

Week 9
Mendelian genetics

Lab 2. Punnett prediction first, counting second, χ² last. Then hand them Mendel's own dihybrid numbers and let them notice the fit is suspiciously good — a door into what honest data looks like.

Week 10
Hardy–Weinberg

Lab 5 on a class census. The teaching move is refusing to let "it doesn't fit" be a failure: a departure from equilibrium is the finding. Which assumption broke, and how would you check?

Week 11
Independent investigation

Groups choose a question and a method from anything they've used. They pre-register it as an .eco protocol — hypothesis committed before the run — and the engine grades the prediction, not the vibe.

Week 12
Present and defend

Every page in the kit prints clean, so a report is a printout plus an argument. Groups present; the audience's job is to find the weakest inference. Give points for finding one.

Why the game comes second. It's tempting to save Field Season as a reward for the end of term. Don't. It's the cheapest possible way to make a class feel the cost of a bad sampling plan, and everything after week 2 is easier because they've already paid that cost once where it didn't count.

Students who have been wrong on the record, early, argue better for the rest of the term.

Four

Thirty students, six tablets

The real constraint is rarely the science. It's that you have half the devices you'd like and forty-five minutes that includes getting coats on.

Group size

Three is the number. One student taps, one calls out what they see, one keeps time and watches for mistakes — and they rotate roles each session so nobody spends the term as the secretary. Pairs work but leave nobody to catch errors. Fours reliably produce one passenger.

Thirty students, groups of three, is ten devices. If you have six, run six field groups and four analysis groups and swap them halfway: the analysis groups work last session's data in the workbench while the field groups collect. It also means two rounds of data instead of one, which makes the pooling step below far more interesting.

The pooling move

This is the highest-value ten minutes in the whole kit, and it's easy to skip. Every app exports a CSV. Have each group paste theirs into one shared sheet, then put the pooled numbers on the board next to each group's own.

  • Small samples swing wildly; the pooled estimate doesn't. Students see sample size working instead of being told about it.
  • Outlier groups become a conversation instead of an embarrassment: why did group 4 get double? Different patch, or different counting rule?
  • It's how real field data gets made, which is the point.

Tablets in the field

  • Load the page indoors before you leave. Once it's open it needs no signal.
  • Turn on airplane mode. Doubles the battery and removes the notification problem.
  • Fill in the Session card first — site, observer, date. It rides onto every export, and six untitled exports at 3pm are indistinguishable from each other.
  • Screen brightness all the way up. The warm paper palette is readable in sun; the default half-brightness is not.
  • One device per group, not per student. The arguing is the learning.
The one thing that loses data. Only Pheno Tracker saves between sessions; the other apps hold everything in the page. A closed tab is gone. Make "copy the export before you close anything" the last step of every field session, and paste it somewhere — a shared doc, an email to yourself, anywhere. Every app has a Copy button for exactly this reason.
Five

Prep checklists

Photocopy these. They are the difference between a field day and a field trip.

The week before

  • Walk the site yourself. Ten minutes. Is there actually anything living in it?
  • Run the lab once, alone, on your own phone — the whole thing, including the export.
  • Check the weather and pick your rain-day alternative now, not on the morning.
  • Buy the consumables: dried beans (Lab 4), coat hangers (Lab 3). That's the list.
  • Print the data sheets from the Lab Manual — every lab has one and they're built to print.

The day before

  • Charge the tablets. All of them. Yes, including that one.
  • Open the lab page on each device once so it's cached.
  • Bend the quadrats. A bent coat hanger is a quadrat; a hula hoop is a quadrat.
  • Decide group membership yourself. Self-selected groups on a field day cost you fifteen minutes.
  • Write the sampling rule on the board — where quadrats go, how long a focal sample runs. Decided in advance, it's a method; decided in the field, it's a bias.

Ten minutes before

  • Airplane mode on, brightness up, page already open.
  • Session card filled: site, observer, date.
  • Everyone can state the question in one sentence before anyone counts anything.
  • Name the stopping rule — "we come back at ten past" — out loud.
Six

Assessment

The kit computes the numbers, which means you cannot grade arithmetic any more — and shouldn't have been. Grade the three things the tool can't do for them.

Rubric 1 — Data quality

Did they collect something worth analysing?

LevelWhat it looks like
ExemplaryMethod stated before collection and followed. Sampling rule explicit enough that another group could repeat it. Session metadata complete. Anomalies noted at the time — "quadrat 6 was half on the path" — rather than quietly dropped.
ProficientMethod stated and mostly followed. Data complete and legible. Minor gaps acknowledged.
DevelopingMethod emerged during collection rather than before it. Data complete but unrepeatable — you couldn't tell from the sheet where the quadrats went.
BeginningData thin or invented. Suspiciously round numbers, or a tally that doesn't match the time spent.

Rubric 2 — Interpretation

Do they know what their own number means?

LevelWhat it looks like
ExemplaryStates the number, says what it means biologically, and names a mechanism that would produce it. Distinguishes what the data show from what they suspect. Notices when two indices disagree and explains why they can.
ProficientReports and correctly interprets the number. Offers a plausible biological explanation.
DevelopingReports the number and re-states the tool's sentence in their own words without adding biology.
BeginningReports the number with no interpretation, or an interpretation the number doesn't support.

Rubric 3 — Honest uncertainty

This is the one worth the most marks.

LevelWhat it looks like
ExemplaryNames the specific limitation that matters most for this result — not a generic "we could have used more samples." Says which assumption is shakiest and what they'd measure next to test it. Reports a result that went against their prediction without dressing it up.
ProficientIdentifies real limitations relevant to their method. Doesn't overclaim.
DevelopingGeneric limitations that would apply to any experiment.
BeginningClaims certainty the data can't carry, or hides a result that didn't work.

What an A actually looks like

Two students, same lab, same site. Here is the difference, in their own closing sentences:

A solid B"The verge had higher diversity (H′ = 1.57) than the lawn (H′ = 1.05), because the lawn is mowed and the verge isn't. More species can survive in the verge."
An A"The verge had higher H′ (1.57 vs 1.05), but almost all of that gap is evenness, not richness — five species against four. The lawn's problem isn't that species are missing, it's that grass is 61% of it. If mowing is the cause I'd expect the difference to shrink at the unmowed edge, which is the thing I'd measure next. Our two sites were also sampled twenty minutes apart in different light, which I don't think matters for plants but would for the pollinator count we did after."

The A doesn't have better data or more words. It decomposes its own number, commits to a testable consequence, and volunteers a weakness nobody asked about. That's the behaviour to reward, and once you've read one aloud, the rest of the class starts producing it.

The pre-registration option. For a graded investigation, have groups write an .eco protocol that commits to a hypothesis before they collect — the engine then grades the prediction against the result. It removes the temptation to discover the hypothesis after seeing the data, which is a habit worth breaking early and a real problem in professional science too.
Seven

When it goes wrong

It will. Most of these are better lessons than the plan was.

It's raining

Swap to Lab 6 (island), Lab 7 (food webs), or Lab 5 (Hardy–Weinberg) — none need to go outside. Or run Field Season, where losing days to weather is the mechanic and the lesson. Say out loud that this is what field seasons are actually like; it isn't a consolation prize, it's the discipline.

A group's data is obviously wrong

Four hundred aphids on one leaf, or a tally that couldn't have happened in ten minutes. Don't discard it quietly — that teaches exactly the wrong thing. Put it on the board with the others and ask the room: what would have to be true for this to be right? Usually the group finds the answer themselves (they counted a colony as individuals; they double-tapped). A group that diagnoses its own bad data has learned more than a group whose data was fine.

A group finished in fifteen minutes

They sampled too little, and the fix is a question, not more worksheets: how confident are you? Would another three quadrats change your answer? Send them to find out. If they come back with a changed answer, they've discovered sampling effort by themselves. If it doesn't change, they've discovered saturation — also a real result, and they can now say why they're confident.

The numbers don't fit the expected ratio

Good. This is the single best teaching moment the kit produces, and the instinct to treat it as a failed lab is the one to resist. A χ² that rejects 3:1 means something: linkage, a mis-scored phenotype, a small sample, or a real biological surprise. Make them argue for one and say how they'd distinguish it from the others. The tool states the small-sample caveat itself, which gives you an opening.

Two groups at the same site got different answers

Almost always sampling variance, occasionally a real difference in microhabitat, sometimes different counting rules. Work through those three in order as a class. This is the pooling conversation and it's worth more than either group's individual result.

A student loses their data

Closed the tab, no export. It happens once per class per term. It's recoverable as a lesson — have them redo a short version and they will never skip an export again — but you can prevent most of it by making "copy the export" the ritual last step. See the warning in section four.

Eight

Reaching everyone

The student who's struggling

Give them the tally role first — it's concrete, it's essential, and it produces something the group visibly needs. The apps are tap-only with no typing and no arithmetic, so the barrier to contributing is genuinely low. Then move them to interpretation with a scaffold: "the number is ___, which means ___, and I think that's because ___." Most students who "can't do the maths" can do the science once the maths stops being the gate.

The student who's ahead

Don't give them more counting. Give them a harder question:

  • Take the CSV into a spreadsheet or R and check the tool's number by hand.
  • Run the same site twice a week apart and argue about what changed.
  • In the food-web builder, find the minimum set of removals that collapses the web.
  • In Field Season, beat their own score on the same season number — same world, better strategy.

The student who's hooked

Point them at the Field Guide for where each method comes from, the essay for why any of this exists, and the .eco format for running a graded experiment of their own design. The Pheno Tracker and Farm Scout are the same science pointed at plant breeding and agriculture — a real answer to "what would I do with this."

Language and access

Every reading in the kit is plain-language first and technical second; the tools narrate results in sentences, not just numbers. Ethogram and species names are student-entered, so they can be in any language. Every page prints clean if a student works better on paper than on a screen.

Nine

Teaching uncertainty honestly

If the kit has a thesis, this is it, and it's the reason the tools are built the way they are.

A textbook lab tends to have a right answer in the back. The student's job is to arrive at it, and a result that misses is a failed lab. This trains a habit that is precisely backwards from science: it teaches that unexpected results mean you did something wrong.

Every lab here is built the other way. The quadrat data is whatever was actually in the grass. The χ² either fits or it doesn't. The knockout takes down whichever species it takes down. There is no back of the book — and so "my result disagrees with my prediction" becomes the start of the interesting part rather than the end of the lab.

A number without a stated uncertainty is a rumour with decimal places.

The tools try to model this rather than just permit it. They state caveats alongside results instead of burying them:

  • The χ² checker names the small-sample problem when your run is small.
  • The breeding bench reports selection differential S and then says plainly that the response you actually get is R = h² × S — and that it can't know your h².
  • The pest scout's action thresholds are labelled placeholders, because real ones are local.
  • The food-web knockout says its count is a lower bound on disruption, not a prediction of what would literally die.
  • Lab 7 carries no .eco pre-registration block, and says why: the format has no food-web keyword, and inventing one that graded nothing would be worse than the gap.

Use those out loud. When a tool volunteers its own limitation, point at it: this is what it looks like when something is honest about what it doesn't know. Then hold student work to the same standard — which is what rubric 3 is for. Students imitate the epistemics they're handed. Hand them good ones.

One phrase worth stealing. When a group reports a result, ask "how would you know if you were wrong?" It works on every lab in this kit, it takes four seconds, and after a few weeks they start answering it before you ask.
Ten

What this kit can't do

Read this before you build a term around it. A guide that only sells you the upside isn't a guide.

  • It isn't a gradebook or an LMS. No accounts, no roster, no submission portal, no analytics on who did what. Students export and hand in; you grade the way you already grade. For a lot of teachers this is a feature — no student data leaves the device, so there's nothing to review with your privacy office — but if you wanted automatic submission tracking, it isn't here.
  • It won't identify species for you. No image recognition, no built-in field guide of local flora. Students name what they see, in whatever words they have. Pair it with a regional guide or a naming key if identification is part of your objective.
  • Most of it doesn't save. Only the Pheno Tracker persists between sessions. The rest lives in the open page, by design — nothing to sync, nothing to leak, nothing to administer — but it means the export step is not optional.
  • It isn't a curriculum. There are no lecture slides, no reading assignments, no homework sets, no answer key. It's laboratory and fieldwork infrastructure; the course around it is yours.
  • The statistics are introductory. χ² goodness-of-fit, diversity indices, Lincoln–Petersen, a selection differential. There's no ANOVA, no regression, no mixed models. That's the right level for an intro course and the wrong level for an undergraduate research project — though the CSV export drops straight into R or Python when a student outgrows it.
  • It's a hand-built project, not a product. No support line, no institutional contract, no guaranteed maintenance window. It's MIT-licensed, so it's yours to keep and modify, and it will keep working offline for as long as browsers open HTML files. But plan accordingly.
What it does do well, to be fair to it: it removes the arithmetic barrier between a student and a real analysis, it makes data collection fast enough to do twice, and it is honest about its own limits in a way that gives you something to teach with.
Last

Start here on Monday

If you've read this far, the smallest real step is one period and no equipment.

  1. Open the Food Web Builder yourself tonight. Tap the pond preset, long-press the minnow, watch what starves. Five minutes.
  2. Print Lab 7 — it prints as a clean handout with its data sheets.
  3. Run it Monday. Groups of three, one device each, the predict-then-knockout procedure.
  4. Ask the closing question and let them fight about it: was the species you removed important, or just common?

If that period goes well, come back to the twelve-week sequence. If it doesn't, you've lost fifty minutes and gained a food-web lesson that didn't need a photocopier.
