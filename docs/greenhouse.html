Environment

VPD, dew point and absolute humidity from your log, against the band for the stage you are in. Time outside the band is measured by duration, not by counting samples.

Which VPD this page shows, and why it matters

There are two numbers called VPD and most published target bands do not say which they mean.

air VPD  = es(Tair) − es(Tair) × RH/100
leaf VPD = es(Tleaf) − es(Tair) × RH/100

This page shows leaf VPD. A transpiring leaf under a lamp sits below air temperature — commonly 1–3 °C below with airflow, less in still air, and it can sit above air temperature under a hot fixture with no movement. A cooler leaf has a lower saturation pressure, so a larger offset gives a lower VPD.

Because es() is exponential, the size of that shift is not small. Swept across 18–32 °C and 40–85% RH, a 2 °C offset moves VPD by 0.25 to 0.51 kPa — median 0.36. The vegetative band is 0.4 kPa wide, so the offset alone is worth 61% to 128% of the whole band: at the top of that range it is wider than the band it is being compared against. That is not a rounding difference. It is the difference between reading “in band” and not, and a target band quoted without its leaf assumption is not a number.

Set the offset to 0 and you get air VPD. If your log carries a measured leaf temperature, the page uses it and ignores the offset entirely — a measurement beats an assumption every time.

Saturation vapour pressure is Buck (Buck, 1981), the equation NOAA publishes and most controllers implement, so a number here matches the number on your controller's own screen. It is within 0.05% of the reference formulation across this whole range.

The target bands are grower convention, not a controlled result. 0.4–0.8 kPa for clones, 0.8–1.2 vegetative, 1.0–1.4 early flower, 1.2–1.6 late flower are the ranges in common circulation among growers and equipment makers. The stage boundaries are soft and the numbers vary between sources. Use them as a starting point and let your own record move them.

Light

DLI is the integral of light over the photoperiod — the number the plant actually experiences. PPFD alone is a snapshot.

DLI, and why it is an integral

DLI = PPFD × hours × 0.0036  mol/m²/d
1 µmol/m²/s × 3600 s/h ÷ 10⁶ µmol/mol = 0.0036

That closed form is only right when PPFD is constant for the whole photoperiod. A dimming ramp, a sunrise curve, a two-stage photoperiod or a cloudy afternoon all break it. When your log carries PPFD, this page integrates the actual curve by trapezoid instead, and reports both so you can see the gap.

Lumens are the wrong unit, and this page will not pretend otherwise

Lumens weight photons by the human photopic response, which peaks at 555 nm — green, the wavelength a leaf reflects most and absorbs least. PPFD counts every photon from 400–700 nm equally. The ratio between them is a property of your fixture's spectrum and nothing else, and across lamps people actually own it spans the whole range of the table below — 24 lux per µmol/m²/s for a red-and-blue LED to 82 for HPS, which is 82 ÷ 24 = 3.4, a factor of more than three.

There is no default factor, so this page does not have one. Give it a factor and it converts, names the factor in the output, and carries that name into the export. Leave it blank and it returns nothing rather than three significant figures invented from a guess. The starting figures below are published approximations for whole lamp classes, not measurements of your lamp; a $30 PAR meter ends the argument permanently.

Energy & yield

What the run cost, what it produced, and the ratio between them — in both of the two numbers that get called “grams per watt”.

Two different numbers share the name “g/W”

g/W   = yield ÷ rated fixture watts
g/kWh = yield ÷ energy actually consumed

The first is the figure the industry quotes. It ignores photoperiod, dimming, and everything in the room that is not the light. A fixture run at 60% for half the cycle has the same g/W as one run flat out and uses 0.60 × 0.50 = 0.30 of the energy, so its g/kWh is 1 ÷ 0.30 = 3.3 times higher. Anyone comparing g/W across two rooms with different duty cycles is comparing nothing at all.

The second is what your meter saw. It is the one that divides into your bill, and it is the one this page leads with.

Energy is integrated, not multiplied

kWh = ∫P dt  ≈  Σ (Pi + Pi−1) / 2 × Δti

When a power log is available the page uses the trapezoid rule over it. Controllers that claim to log every five minutes drop samples under load, and a rectangle sum silently attributes each gap to whichever endpoint it happened to use. The trapezoid is correct for any spacing, which is the only assumption safe to make about real logs.

What this page will not compute. It does not predict a yield, price a harvest, or tell you whether more light would pay. The first needs a controlled trial, the second is not a horticultural question, and the third depends on where you sit on a diminishing-returns curve that only your own record can locate — which is precisely what logging run after run is for.

Where the data comes from

Five ways in. Each one says whether it can run in this browser, on this page, right now — and if it cannot, why not.

Adding a sixth

The page does not know the list. A source is one registration call, and it must answer the same two questions as the rest:

GH.register({
  id:"mine", name:"My rig", kind:"poll",
  available:function(){ return {ok:…, why:"…"}; },
  read:function(opts){ return Promise.resolve({rows:[…]}); }
});

available() is not optional decoration. A monitoring page that fails silently is worse than one that does not load: it shows you an empty room and lets you believe the room is empty. Every source here reports its own limits before you depend on it — a file:// page cannot fetch http://, an https:// page cannot fetch a plain-http sensor, and Web Serial does not exist outside Chromium. Those are all checked, not assumed.
