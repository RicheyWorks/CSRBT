Fungal Characters

The vocabulary a mycological key assumes you already have, drawn rather than defined — the spore print chart, the spot tests, fifty-one genera with the one thing that gives each away, and the six confusions worth being able to settle cold. Built to print and go in the lid of a collecting basket.

One

Working out what you have

The order matters. Characters that vanish are recorded first, characters that keep are recorded last.

A fungus is not a plant, and the sequence a botanist uses does not transfer. Half of what identifies a fruit body is gone within a day of collecting it: latex dries, odour fades, colour changes stop changing, viscidity disappears, and the spore print you did not take cannot be taken later. The other half — where it grew, what it grew on, what tree it grew near — cannot be recovered from the specimen at all, at any point, by anyone.

  1. Record the site before you touch the fungus. Substrate, host tree, habit, and whether it is on wood or merely near wood. A fruit body arising from buried wood looks terrestrial and is not.
  2. Photograph in place, then in section. Include the base and a scale.
  3. Lift the whole fruit body, base included. Do not cut. The volva, the bulb and the rhizomorphs live below the litter line, and they are frequently the deciding characters.
  4. Describe it fresh: cap, margin, hymenophore, gill attachment, stipe, veil remnants, odour, and what the flesh does when cut — latex, colour change, and how fast.
  5. Set a spore print the moment you get back. Hours, not minutes.
  6. Then run spot tests, then key, then dry.
The single most common mistake is keying first and printing afterwards. The print is the hardest macroscopic evidence there is, and it will overturn a key result rather than confirm one. Take it before you commit to a name.

What a herbarium sheet cannot recover, your notes must carry.

Two

Cap, margin, stipe and veil

The shapes a key names, drawn. Shape changes with age — describe what you have and say how old it is.

Cap shape, in profile

Cap shape is a stage as much as a character. An ovoid cap becomes campanulate, then convex, then plane, then depressed, and a key that says “convex” usually means “convex at maturity”. Record the range across the collection rather than one cap.

convexThe commonest shape: a smooth dome, higher than half a sphere is not.
plane / applanateFlat across the top, with at most a slight rise. Often an aged convex cap.
umbonateA distinct raised boss at the centre, like a nipple on a dome.
depressedSunken in the middle but not yet funnel-shaped.
infundibuliformA true funnel — the depression runs down into the stipe.
campanulateBell-shaped: taller than wide, with the margin flaring out.
conicalStraight-sided cone, margin not flared.
ovoidEgg-shaped, margin still pressed to the stipe — a young cap.
umbilicateA small sharp navel at the centre, as if pushed in with a thumb.

The margin

The edge of the cap carries evidence about the veil and about the flesh. Striations mean the flesh is thin enough to see the gills through; appendiculate fragments mean a partial veil tore there.

striateFine radial lines at the edge — you are seeing the gills through thin flesh.
sulcateDeep grooves, not fine lines — the margin is pleated.
appendiculateTorn veil fragments hanging from the cap edge like a fringe.
inrolledThe edge curls under, sometimes for the life of the fruit body.
plane / straightNeither curled nor grooved — the default, and worth stating as an observation.

Stipe and veil

Two veils may be present. A universal veil encloses the whole young fruit body and leaves a volva, a rim, or warts on the cap. A partial veil runs from cap margin to stipe and leaves a ring, a ring zone, or a cortina. They are different structures with different diagnostic weight, and the universal veil is the one that is buried.

volvaA sac at the base, left by a universal veil. It is often under the duff — dig, do not cut.
bulbous with a rimA swollen base finished with a distinct gutter or flange.
membranous ringA skirt of tissue left where the partial veil tore away from the cap margin.
cortinaA cobweb of fine threads from cap margin to stipe — gone within a day.
ring zoneNo skirt, only a band of fibres — and often rusty spore dust caught in it.
scabersTufted dark points on a pale stipe. In the field this alone says Leccinum.
reticulateA raised net over the stipe surface, not a pattern of dots.
rooting pseudorhizaA carrot-like extension going down into the soil — collect it or lose the character.
lateral / absent stipeThe cap attaches at its side, or directly to the wood. Shelving fungi.
Dig, do not cut. A collection taken with a knife at the litter line is missing the character that separates the most dangerous genus in the northern hemisphere from its harmless neighbours. Lift the whole thing, then put the duff back.

Three

The spore-bearing surface

What is under the cap, and how it meets the stipe. This is where keys start.

Hymenophore type

The first branch of almost every macrofungal key. Feel it as well as look at it: the difference between a gill and a fold, and between a bolete's tubes and a polypore's, is textural.

gillsBlades hanging from the flesh, thin and knife-like in section.
pores / tubesA layer of tubes; you see their mouths. Boletes have soft separable tubes, polypores hard fused ones.
teeth / spinesFree-hanging spines, each one tapering to a point.
ridges / false gillsBlunt forking folds, thicker than a gill and running into each other.
smooth or veinedNo projections at all — at most shallow wrinkles. Chanterelle relatives, crusts, cups.
maze-like / daedaloidPores stretched and joined into winding slots — between pores and gills.
enclosed glebaThe spore mass matures sealed inside — puffballs, earthstars, false truffles.

Gill attachment

Slice the fruit body vertically through the stipe and look at the junction from the side. Attachment changes as the cap expands, so read it on a mature but not collapsing specimen, and say which.

freeThe gills stop short of the stipe — a clear gutter runs all the way round.
adnexedGills reach the stipe but touch it only by a narrow point.
adnateGills meet the stipe squarely, along their full depth.
sinuate / emarginateA notch just before the stipe, then a short tooth running down it.
decurrentGills run down the stipe instead of stopping at it.
secedingAttached when young, then torn free, leaving a mark on the stipe.
Gill spacing, edge and colour are worth recording alongside attachment: crowded against distant; entire against serrate or fringed; and whether the edge is a different colour from the face. The last of those is often a genus-level character and it is easy to miss without a lens.

Four

The spore print

The single most informative macroscopic character on an agaric, and the one most often skipped.

Cut the stipe flush, set the cap gills-down on paper, cover it with a bowl to stop draughts, and leave it for several hours — overnight is normal. Half black and half white paper reads both pale and dark prints. A thin print, or a print from a young cap, reads paler than the truth, so judge the colour where the deposit is heaviest, in daylight, against white.

For a bolete, stand a slice of the tube layer on edge rather than printing the whole cap. For a Coprinopsis, print it immediately or not at all — the gills are dissolving as you carry it.

whiteAmanita, Tricholoma, Hygrophorus, Clitocybe, Marasmius, Mycena, Lepiota, Armillaria, Laccaria, most polypores
cream / pale buffRussula and Lactarius run white through cream to deep ochre, and the exact shade is a key character in both — match it against a printed chart, not against memory.
lilac-greyPleurotus can throw a faintly lilac print on white paper. Easy to miss and easy to over-read.
pink / salmonPluteus on wood, Entoloma on soil, Volvariella with a volva, Clitopilus with decurrent gills. A pink print puts you in a small, well-bounded set.
rusty ochreCortinarius above all, plus Gymnopilus and Galerina on wood and Phaeocollybia with its rooting base.
clay-brownHebeloma, and Inocybe at the duller end. Clay is not rust and not chocolate; judge it on white.
olive-brownThe boletes: Boletus, Suillus, Leccinum. Take the print by standing a tube slice on paper, not a whole cap.
brownCrepidotus on wood with no stipe at all; Ganoderma dusts everything below it brown, which is often how you find it.
purple-brownHypholoma and Stropharia. Against black paper it reads dark; against white the purple shows.
chocolateAgaricus. Free gills that go pink then chocolate, and a ring — the print settles it.
blackCoprinopsis and Coprinellus as the gills dissolve, Panaeolus from mottled gills, Gomphidius from decurrent ones.
dull greenChlorophyllum molybdites. A lepiotoid mushroom on a lawn whose mature print is green is that until proved otherwise, and it is the most common cause of mushroom poisoning in North America.
When the print contradicts the key, the print wins. A key result is an inference from characters you judged. A print is a physical measurement of the thing that defines the fungus. If they disagree, re-key from the print.

Five

Macrochemical spot tests

Reagents separate species within a genus. Almost none of them separate genera, and none identifies anything alone.

A spot test is a character like any other — it narrows a set, and it is worth nothing without the substrate, the print and the morphology beside it. Record where you put the drop, what happened, and how fast. Blank is not negative: a test you did not run and a test that produced no reaction are different results and belong in different columns.

Handling. KOH is caustic and will damage an eye instantly. Phenol is toxic by skin absorption and by vapour. Melzer's contains chloral hydrate and is controlled in some jurisdictions. Use dropper bottles rather than open dishes, wear eye protection, work with ventilation, and never carry reagents loose in a basket with your specimens.

Six

Work out the genus

Answer what you can see. Leave anything you are unsure of blank — the ranking gets better with more answers, not with guesses.

Dead giveaways — tap any you noticed

The key ranks fifty-one genera and genus groups on set membership: a character that matches adds, a dead giveaway adds more, and a character that contradicts subtracts. It is deliberately forgiving, because field characters are read imperfectly — which also means a top rank is a hypothesis to test against a flora or a monograph, never an identification. When a genus is ruled out, the key names the character that ruled it out, so you can go back and check whether you read that character correctly.

Seven

Fifty-one genera on sight

One line each: the character that gives it away in the field, before you open a key at all.

A tell is a starting point, not a determination. Each of these gets you to a genus quickly and honestly. Getting from a genus to a species is a different job, and for a good third of the genera above it is a job for a microscope — see section nine.

Eight

The pairs that get confused

Six separations worth being able to make cold. Two of them are the ones that put people in hospital.

Amanita against Agaricus and the lepiotoids

All three can be a white mushroom with free gills and a ring on a stipe.

how to settle it

Take a print and dig the base. Agaricus prints chocolate and has no volva. Lepiotoids print white (or green in Chlorophyllum) and have a sliding ring on a smooth stipe. Amanita prints white and has a universal veil at the base — a sac, a rim, or warts — which is frequently buried. A knife-cut collection throws that character away, and it is the character that matters most in the whole of mycology.

Gills against false gills against folds

Chanterelles are called chanterelles because of what is under the cap, and people read it wrong.

how to settle it

A true gill is a thin blade with two free faces; run a fingernail across it and it breaks cleanly. A false gill is a blunt fold of the cap flesh itself, forking and running into its neighbours; it deforms rather than breaks. Cantharellus and Craterellus have folds. Hygrophoropsis has true gills, and so do the Omphalotus species that are confused with them.

Bolete against polypore

Both have pores. The distinction is textural, not visual.

how to settle it

In a bolete the tube layer is soft and lifts cleanly away from the cap flesh as a mat. In a polypore the tubes are fused to a tough or woody context and cannot be separated. Boletes are almost all terrestrial and ectomycorrhizal; polypores are almost all on wood and saprotrophic or parasitic. Albatrellus is the fleshy stalked exception that proves the rule.

Morchella against Gyromitra and Verpa

Three spring ascomycetes that a photograph will not separate.

how to settle it

Slice it lengthwise. Morchella is one clean hollow from the tip of the pitted head to the base of the stipe, and the head is fused to the stipe along its length. Verpa hangs from the stipe apex like a thimble and the stipe is stuffed with cottony tissue. Gyromitra is convoluted rather than pitted and the interior is chambered. Gyromitrin, in the last of those, is not neutralised by any reliable field or kitchen procedure.

Galerina against anything small and brown on wood

Small brown mushrooms on wood are the classic hard case, and one of the genera in that group carries amatoxins.

how to settle it

This is the situation where a field key stops being useful. A rusty-brown print, a ring, and a wood substrate narrows it, but the genus contains species separated on microscopic characters. Collect it, print it, dry it, and take it to someone with a scope.

Chlorophyllum molybdites on a lawn

It looks like a parasol mushroom and is the most frequent cause of mushroom poisoning in North America.

how to settle it

The separating character is the mature spore print, which is dull green rather than white — and it will read white from a young cap. Any large scaly lepiotoid from a lawn or a park gets a full-length print from a mature cap before it gets a name.

Nothing on this card tells you whether a fungus is edible. There is no edibility column, and that is a decision rather than an oversight. The genera that kill people sit alongside harmless ones and are separated by characters — a volva under the duff, a green tint in a mature print, an amyloid reaction on a slide — that a printed card cannot verify on your behalf. People are poisoned in the gap between “the key came out at X” and “therefore I ate it”, and that gap does not close by reading more carefully. Use this card to identify. Take the eating question to a regional expert with the specimen in hand, and to a poison centre if anyone has already eaten something.

Nine

Collecting, drying, and where the field stops

How to make a specimen that is still worth something in ten years, and an honest account of what this card cannot do.

Collecting

A fruit body is the reproductive organ of a mycelium that may be decades old and hectares wide, so picking it is closer to picking an apple than to pulling a plant. The litter layer it sits in is not: raking duff damages the thing you came to study. Lift the whole fruit body including the stipe base, put the duff back, and leave undersized and overmature bodies where they are. Take what you will actually describe and dry — a basket that rots in the car is a loss with nothing to set against it.

Get permission on private land. Check the rules before collecting in a National Park, a Wilderness or a research natural area, where collecting is often restricted or prohibited outright. Record locality precisely enough that the collection can be relocated, and blur or withhold coordinates publicly for rare taxa.

Drying

Fungi rot faster than they dry unless you help. Halve or slice anything thicker than a thumb, spread it in a single layer, and run air over it warm rather than hot.

  • 40–45 °C with airflow is the usual working compromise. It is a starting point to adapt, not a standard.
  • Above about 50 °C the DNA in the tissue degrades and the specimen cooks rather than dries. If you may ever want to sequence it, stay below that.
  • Below about 35 °C a fleshy cap can spoil before it is dry.
  • Take a DNA subsample into silica before drying if you can. It costs a minute and it is the difference between a specimen that can be sequenced cleanly and one that cannot.
  • Dry it until it snaps rather than bends, then seal it against reabsorption and against insects.

The label

The label carries what the dried specimen lost. At minimum: collector, number, date, locality with coordinates and elevation, habitat and the dominant trees, substrate and host, habit, fresh dimensions, odour, colour changes on cutting, spore print colour, any spot tests, and the determiner. A dried Russula without a note of its print colour and its iron reaction is close to worthless; with them it is a usable record.

Where the field stops

A large share of the genera on this card cannot be taken to species in the field by anyone. Cortinarius, Inocybe, Russula, Entoloma, Mycena, Galerina and most crusts need spore measurements and ornamentation, the amyloid or dextrinoid reaction in Melzer's, cystidia, pileipellis structure, clamp connections — and increasingly, sequencing. Several of them contain species complexes that were only separated once sequencing arrived, which means field guides older than about fifteen years apply names that have since been split.

That is not a reason to stop at the genus. It is a reason to write cf. or aff. in front of a species epithet and let the determination happen where it can be made. Everything this card teaches you to record is what makes that later determination possible.

The companion app. Collection Sheet records all of this on a tablet in the field — substrate and host, habit, hymenophore, veil characters, the spore print with a colour picker, spot tests, drying log — and computes richness, Chao1, guild spectrum and a host association matrix from the collections. It carries no edibility field either.
