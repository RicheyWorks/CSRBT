Four published recipes, transcribed and scalable

Super Soil, TLO, Coot's and the BuildASoil mix — each with its author, its source, its cook time and its own failure mode. Pick a batch size and the quantities scale.

These are grower conventions, not agronomy. Every recipe here was published by a named grower on a forum or in a book, and every one is widely used and widely reported to work. None of them rests on a soil test, a control or a replicated trial, and the quantities differ between printings of the same recipe. They are reproduced exactly as published, with the source linked, so you can go and read the argument rather than take a number on trust. This page will not tell you which is best, because nothing it has access to could answer that.

What each amendment actually does

The column that matters is release speed. It is why a mix is left to cook, why blood meal burns a seedling and rock phosphate never does, and why a run that goes hungry in week seven was usually built without anything slow in it.

The NPK figures are typical published ranges for the material, not measurements of your bag. Every one varies with source — published C:N for “leaves” alone spans 40 to 90 — and a bag's own label beats this table every time. Use the column to reason about direction, never as an input to a calculation.

Reading a recipe before you build it

Hot mixes and mild mixes are two different designs

Subcool's and the Rev's are hot: concentrated amendment, banded or layered so the roots meet it on a schedule, and enough nitrogen in one place to kill a seedling planted into it. Coot's and the BuildASoil mix are mild: everything is dilute and even, the container is meant to be re-used, and feeding happens by top-dressing later. Neither design is wrong. Mixing the two — a hot band under a mild mix, or a mild mix fed as though it were hot — is where most of the trouble comes from.

Cooking is not a ritual

The wait exists because the fast amendments have to be converted by soil organisms before roots can use them, and because that conversion produces ammonia and heat. Thirty days for Subcool's, fifteen for the Rev's, four to six weeks for Coot's: those numbers come from the recipes themselves. Keep the pile damp, not wet. A mix that smells of ammonia is not finished.

Calcium is the amendment people double up on without noticing

Dolomite lime, agricultural lime, oyster shell flour and gypsum all supply calcium, and three of the four also raise pH. Several of these recipes carry two or three of them at once. Gypsum is the one that does not move pH. If you are working from hard water, read the lime line of your recipe before you follow it.

Where this page stops

It does not compute a C:N ratio, a nutrient total or a predicted yield from these recipes. The published quantities are volumes and weights of materials whose analyses vary by a factor of two, so any total computed from them would carry an error bar wider than the answer. The Soil Bench will compute a C:N on dry mass if you have real figures for your own ingredients, which is the only way that number means anything.

Grow what is legal where you are. Cannabis law varies by country, state and municipality, and it changes. Nothing on this page is advice about whether you may grow anything. Some amendments here — neem and karanja above all — are regulated as pesticides in many jurisdictions regardless of what you grow.
