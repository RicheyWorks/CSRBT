Lab 1

Behavioral Observation: Ethograms & Activity Budgets

For: animal-behavior students, field assistants · setting: any observable animal (campus squirrels, a feeder, an enclosure, video) · ~60–90 min

You will be able to:
  • Build an ethogram — a catalogue of defined, mutually-exclusive behaviors.
  • Sample behavior with a named method (focal-animal and scan sampling) and know when each is honest.
  • Turn tallies into an activity budget and read its behavioral diversity and evenness.

Background

An ethogram is the vocabulary of a behavioral study: a short list of behaviors, each defined so precisely that two observers score the same act the same way. How you sample matters as much as what you see (Altmann 1974). In focal-animal sampling you fix on one individual and record everything it does for a set window; in scan sampling you sweep the whole group at fixed instants and record what each animal is doing right then. Focal sampling gives true time budgets and sequences but only for one animal at a time; scan sampling covers the group but misses brief acts. An activity budget is the proportion of time (or of scan points) spent in each behavior — and because it is just an abundance distribution over categories, the same diversity mathematics that describes a meadow describes a behavioral repertoire: Shannon H′ (Shannon 1948) measures how varied the behavior is, and Pielou's J′ (Pielou 1966) whether time is spread evenly or dominated by one activity.

What you need (field kit)

  • A clipboard or phone, a timer/stopwatch, and a pre-written ethogram (4–8 behaviors).
  • A tally sheet (the data sheet below) — one column per behavior.
  • A subject you can watch without disturbing it; decide your sampling rule before you start.
📓
Collect it on a tablet. The Field Notebook's Ethogram tab is this lab's tally sheet: one big button per behavior, a scan-sample timer that beeps and buzzes at your interval, undo for the miscount, and a live activity budget with H′ and J′ as you tap. Long-press a card to fix a behavior's name without losing its count. Fill the Session card so site, observer, and date land on the export.

Procedure

  1. Define the ethogram. Agree on 4–8 behaviors and a one-line definition for each (e.g. forage, rest, groom, vigilance, locomote, social). Ambiguity here is the biggest source of error.
  2. Choose a method. For one animal's time budget, do focal sampling for a fixed 10-minute window, noting each behavior change. For a group snapshot, do scan sampling every 30 s for 10 minutes.
  3. Score. Make a tally mark in the matching column each time you record a behavior (focal: each change; scan: each animal at each scan).
  4. Compute the activity budget. In the workbench Field data — species counts, enter one line per behavior as behavior count (or paste your tally marks straight in). Read off the proportions, Shannon H′, and evenness J′. The tool narrates it: "uneven — a few hot keys carry most of the traffic" means one behavior dominates the budget.
  5. Compare two contexts (optional). Score the same animal in two settings (fed vs. unfed, morning vs. afternoon) and put each budget into Compare two sites to get Bray–Curtis dissimilarity — how much the budget shifted.
Teaching note. Inter-observer reliability is the lesson hiding here: have two students score the same 10 minutes independently, then compare their two budgets in Compare two sites. A high Bray–Curtis between observers means the ethogram needs tighter definitions, not the animal.

Analysis & questions

  1. Which behavior dominated the budget? Is J′ closer to 1 (even) or 0 (dominated) — and does that match what you saw?
  2. Would focal and scan sampling of the same animal give the same budget? Which brief behaviors does scan sampling systematically miss?
  3. If two contexts differed, what is the Bray–Curtis between their budgets, and what biological factor best explains the shift?

Lab 2

Mendelian Genetics: Peas & Fruit Flies

For: intro genetics students · works for a virtual cross, a Drosophila vial count, or a pea-plant dataset · ~60 min

You will be able to:
  • Predict offspring ratios with a Punnett square for monohybrid, dihybrid, and test crosses.
  • Tally an F2 phenotype count and test it against the expected ratio with a χ² goodness-of-fit test.
  • Recognise incomplete dominance and two-gene interaction when the classic ratios don't appear.

Background

Mendel crossed true-breeding pea plants and counted the offspring (Mendel 1866). A single trait (seed shape) in an F1 × F1 cross gives the famous 3:1 ratio; two traits at once give 9:3:3:1. His actual seed-shape count was 5474 round to 1850 wrinkled — and the point of the lab is not to admire the ratio but to test your own counts against it. The tool for that is the χ² goodness-of-fit test: it asks whether the difference between what you counted and what the ratio predicts is small enough to be chance. Fruit flies (Drosophila) run the same logic on a fast life cycle — a monohybrid vestigial-wing cross or a dihybrid body-colour × wing cross gives the same 3:1 and 9:3:3:1 you can put a χ² on. Not every cross is 3:1: when heterozygotes look like neither parent (the blue Andalusian chicken) you have incomplete dominance and a 1:2:1 phenotype ratio; when two genes interact (comb shape) you get modified dihybrid ratios.

What you need

  • Your cross's parent genotypes, and an F2 phenotype tally — from a real vial/plot count, a class dataset, or a simulated cross.
  • The workbench Punnett builder — the greenhouse & the coop.
🌱
Counting at the bench? The Pheno Tracker's Segregation tab runs the same χ² against 3:1, 1:1, 9:7, 13:3 and 15:1 from two tallied counts — useful when you are scoring vials or plants in front of you rather than typing a finished dataset. It states the small-run caveat with the verdict.

Procedure

  1. Predict. In the Punnett builder, set parent 1 and parent 2 to your genotypes — one letter-pair per gene: Rr for a monohybrid, RrYy for a dihybrid, Aa × aa for a test cross. The square and the expected ratio appear instantly.
  2. Try the famous ones. The preset menu loads them exactly: Mendel's peas: seed shape (Rr × Rr, with his 1866 counts), Mendel's dihybrid: shape × colour (RrYy × RrYy, 315 108 101 32), the Blue Andalusian (tick incomplete dominance), the walnut-comb two-gene cross, and a test cross.
  3. Test your data. Type your own F2 counts into observed counts (space-separated, in the ratio's order). The tool computes χ² and tells you whether to reject the ratio. Mendel's 5474 1850 against 3:1 is the worked example.
  4. Interpret dominance. If your F2 shows three phenotypes in ~1:2:1 rather than 3:1, re-run with incomplete dominance ticked and compare.
Fly-room tip. The white-eye gene in Drosophila is X-linked, so a reciprocal cross gives different results in sons and daughters — count the sexes separately and run each as its own cross. For an autosomal trait (vestigial wings, ebony body), a straight F1 × F1 monohybrid and the 3:1 χ² is the right model.

Lab 3

Biodiversity & Community: Quadrats & Diversity

For: ecology students · setting: a lawn, forest floor, tide pool, or leaf-litter tray · ~90 min incl. fieldwork

You will be able to:
  • Sample a community with quadrats and measure its spatial dispersion (random / regular / clumped).
  • Compute species richness, Shannon diversity, and evenness from your counts.
  • Compare two habitats with β-diversity (Jaccard, Sørensen, Bray–Curtis).

Background

A quadrat is a fixed-area frame you drop to count what's inside — the oldest quantitative method in field ecology. Laid across a site, the counts per quadrat reveal spatial pattern: a variance-to-mean ratio near 1 is random, below 1 is regular (even spacing), above 1 is clumped (Morisita 1959). Pooling the counts gives the community's diversity: richness (how many species), Shannon H′ (richness weighted by evenness), and Pielou's J′. To compare two habitats you use β-diversity: Jaccard and Sørensen ask what fraction of species are shared; Bray–Curtis weighs abundances too.

What you need

  • A quadrat (a bent coat-hanger square, a hula-hoop, or a taped 0.5 m frame) and a field notebook.
  • Two sites to compare, and a rule for placing quadrats (random or on a transect) fixed before you start.
  • The workbench Quadrat grid — dispersion, Field data — species counts, and Compare two sites.
📓
Collect it on a tablet. The Field Notebook carries this whole lab outdoors: Quadrats for the per-frame counts (with a dispersion verdict as you go) and Species for the site tallies, both reading out live. Your day exports as .eco lines and as CSV for Excel or R — so the numbers you paste into the workbench are the ones you actually counted.

Procedure

  1. Place and count. Drop the quadrat at your placement points (aim for ≥10 per site). Record how many individuals of each species fall inside each quadrat.
  2. Dispersion. Put your per-quadrat totals (one number per quadrat) into Quadrat grid. Read the variance/mean ratio and the Morisita index — is the community random, regular, or clumped?
  3. Diversity. Pool the counts per species and enter them as species count lines in Field data. Record richness, H′, and J′.
  4. Compare. Do the same at the second site, then paste both species lists into Compare two sites. Record Jaccard, Sørensen, and Bray–Curtis.

  3. Your two sites share many species but one is dominated by a single one. How do Sørensen (presence only) and Bray–Curtis (abundance) disagree, and why?

Lab 4

Population Size: Mark & Recapture

For: field-methods students · setting: beans in a bag, woodlice under boards, a pond-net survey, or a simulation · ~45 min

You will be able to:
  • Estimate an unseen population size with the Lincoln–Petersen index.
  • State the method's assumptions and predict how breaking each one biases the estimate.

Background

You can't count every animal, so you count twice and use the overlap. Capture a sample, mark them (M), release them, and later capture a second sample (C) — the number in it already marked (R) tells you how big the whole population is. If marked animals mixed back in evenly, the marked fraction of the second catch equals the marked fraction of the population, giving the Lincoln–Petersen estimate N ≈ (M × C) / R. It rests on assumptions: the population is closed (no births, deaths, or movement between catches), marks don't fall off or change behaviour, and marked animals mix back in randomly.

What you need

  • A "population" you can sample twice — a bag of dried beans (mark with a marker), or a real closed site.
  • The workbench Mark–recapture calculator.
📓
Collect it on a tablet. The Field Notebook's More tab has M / C / R steppers with a live Lincoln–Petersen estimate — hold a button to run the count up fast. For practice before the real thing, Field Season hides a vole population behind a twelve-day budget and grades the estimate you file against the truth.

Procedure

  1. First catch. Grab a handful, count and mark them (M), return them, and mix well.
  2. Second catch. Grab another handful. Count the total caught (C) and how many carry a mark (R).
  3. Estimate. Enter M, C, R in the Mark–recapture calculator and read the population estimate. If you know the true count (you can, with beans), compare.
  4. Break an assumption. Repeat but mix poorly, or add unmarked beans between catches, and watch the estimate bias.

  2. You added unmarked individuals between catches (immigration). Did the estimate go up or down, and why does the math do that?
  3. Name a real animal for which "the population is closed between catches" is a bad assumption, and what you'd do instead.

Lab 5

Population Genetics: Hardy–Weinberg

For: evolution students · setting: a class genotype census (PTC tasting, ear lobes) or a published dataset · ~45 min

You will be able to:
  • Estimate allele frequencies from a genotype census.
  • Test whether a population is in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium and interpret a departure.

Background

In an idealised population — large, randomly mating, no selection, mutation, or migration — allele frequencies stay put and genotype frequencies settle at p² : 2pq : q². That is the null model evolution is measured against: if p is the frequency of allele A and q of a, then a population at equilibrium has p² homozygous dominant, 2pq heterozygous, q² homozygous recessive. Counting the three genotypes lets you estimate p and q and then test whether the observed genotype counts match the p²:2pq:q² expectation with a χ². A significant departure is the interesting result — it says one of the idealised conditions is being broken.

What you need

  • A genotype census — counts of the three genotypes (AA, Aa, aa) from a class survey or a dataset.
  • The workbench Genotype census — Hardy–Weinberg.

Procedure

  1. Census. Tally your three genotype counts. (For a recessive phenotype like PTC non-tasting, aa is directly countable; the class can estimate the rest.)
  2. Compute. Enter the three counts in Genotype census. Read the estimated allele frequencies p and q, the expected genotype counts, and the χ² against equilibrium.
  3. Interpret. Decide whether the population is at equilibrium, and if not, which assumption is the likeliest culprit.

  3. Why is Hardy–Weinberg most useful as a null model — i.e., why is the departure more scientifically interesting than the fit?

Island Biogeography: the MacArthur–Wilson Equilibrium

For: ecology & biogeography students · setting: the Interactive Lab's live island (no fieldwork needed) · ~45 min

You will be able to:
  • Explain equilibrium species richness as the crossing of an immigration curve and an extinction curve.
  • Predict — then measure — the area effect (bigger island, more species) and the turnover signature (richness flat while composition churns).
  • Run the Levins/island theory bench in an .eco protocol and compare model to measurement.

Background

MacArthur and Wilson's insight (1967) was that an island's species count is not a fixed inventory but a dynamic equilibrium. New species arrive from the mainland at an immigration rate that falls as the island fills (fewer arrivals are new); resident species go extinct at a rate that rises as more are packed in. Where the two curves cross, richness Ŝ holds steady — but the identity of the residents keeps changing. That churn at constant richness is the theory's signature, and its two famous predictions follow from moving the curves: a bigger island lowers extinction (curve down → higher Ŝ); a nearer island raises immigration (curve up → higher Ŝ).

The equilibrium, drawn

What you need

  • The Interactive Lab's Terrarium — Island panel (its capacity slider is your island's area), or the .eco island model.

Procedure

  1. Predict first. Write down: what should happen to richness and to turnover when the island's capacity is doubled? Halved?
  2. Baseline. In the Terrarium's island panel, set capacity to 12. Record richness (residents/capacity), immigrations, extinctions, and the turnover reading on the data sheet.
  3. The area experiment. Step capacity through 6, 12, 24 (and further if you like). At each setting, record the same four numbers. Watch the turnover fall as capacity grows — a bigger island loses fewer residents.
  4. The signature. At a fixed capacity, watch the composition churn while richness stays pinned at capacity: the island is "full," yet never the same island twice. Note one resident that appears and disappears.
  5. Model vs. measurement. Run the theory bench with an .eco protocol (below): the island model predicts an equilibrium richness from c and e; factor: area and factor: distance move it exactly as the curves say. Compare the model's Ŝ against what the live island measured — and if they disagree, say why (survey cadence is the usual suspect).

  3. Name a real system that behaves like this island besides an island — a habitat fragment, a gut microbiome, a city's food carts — and say what plays the role of area and distance.

Lab 7

Food Webs: Who Eats Whom, and What Happens When One Is Gone

For: ecology and general-biology students · works from a field survey, a reading, or a local habitat you know · ~50 min, no equipment

You will be able to:
  • Build a food web from feeding observations and assign every species a trophic level.
  • Compute and interpret connectance and food-chain length, and say why real chains are short.
  • Run a knockout experiment — remove one species, count the secondary extinctions — and use the result to argue whether that species is a keystone.
  • Explain why a web can look robust and still collapse from one well-chosen loss.

Background

A food web is a map of feeding links, drawn with the arrow pointing from the food to the eater — the direction the energy travels. Stack the species by how many feeding steps separate them from the producers and you get the familiar pyramid: producers at level 1, herbivores at 2, and so on. The pyramid is short for a physical reason. Lindeman's classic measurement (1942) found that only about a tenth of the energy at one level ends up in the next; after four or five steps there is essentially nothing left to support another predator. That is why "level 8" appears in no real ecosystem.

Webs also differ in how densely they are connected. Connectance is realized links divided by all possible ones, L/S², and published webs mostly land between about 0.05 and 0.3. That number matters because it hints at redundancy: if a consumer has four foods, losing one is an inconvenience; if it has one, losing it is fatal.

Which brings us to the experiment this lab is really about. In 1963–69 Robert Paine did something simple and violent on the rocky shore at Mukkaw Bay: he pried the ochre sea stars off a stretch of coast and threw them into the water, month after month, and watched what happened (Paine 1966, 1969). The mussels the sea star had been eating took over. Within a couple of years the plot fell from fifteen species to about eight. The sea star was not abundant and was not obviously important — and removing it restructured the entire community. Paine called such a species a keystone, after the wedge-shaped stone that holds an arch up while carrying little weight itself.

You cannot pry sea stars off a shore in a fifty-minute class. You can run Paine's logic on a web you built yourself, which is what the knockout test does: delete a species, then repeatedly delete any consumer left with no food path, and count what falls. Those follow-on losses are secondary extinctions, and counting them is the standard way ecologists measure a web's robustness today.

Honest framing. A knockout counts species that lose every food path — the harshest, simplest version of the question. Real communities are messier: survivors switch prey, populations crash without vanishing, and effects ripple through abundances long before anything goes extinct (that softer version is a trophic cascade). Treat the count as a lower bound on disruption, not a prediction of what would literally die.

What you need

  • A habitat you can describe: a pond, a meadow, a hedgerow, a rocky shore, a compost heap — real observations if you have them, a reading or a field guide if you don't.
  • The Food Web Builder — no install, works on a tablet.
🕸️
Build it by tapping. Add each species as producer or consumer, then tap the food first and its eater second to draw the arrow (tap the same pair again to erase). Trophic levels lay themselves out as you go, a consumer with no food source is flagged with a dashed outline, and a long-press on any species runs the knockout test — previewed first, committed with one tap, undone with one. Two starter webs (pond, meadow) get a class moving in seconds.

Procedure

  1. List the cast. Name 8–12 organisms from your habitat and sort each into producer or consumer. Aim for at least two producers — a web resting on one plant is a special case, not a typical one.
  2. Draw the links you can defend. Add an arrow only where you have a reason: you saw it, you read it, or the organism's biology makes it near-certain. Write the reason next to the link on your data sheet. Guessed arrows inflate connectance and make the web look more robust than it is.
  3. Record the baseline. Note species (S), links (L), connectance, longest chain, and which species sit at the top with nothing eating them.
  4. Predict before you break. Pick three species: one you expect to matter enormously, one you expect not to matter, and one you're unsure about. Write down, before testing, how many secondary extinctions each will cause.
  5. Run the knockouts. Long-press each in turn, record the actual count, then undo. Every prediction you got wrong is the interesting part of this lab — keep those.
  6. Find the keystone. Which removal did the most damage relative to how unremarkable the species seemed? That mismatch — small presence, large consequence — is the keystone signature.
  7. Stress the web (optional). Add one plausible missing link to your least-connected consumer and re-run its predator's knockout. Notice how a single extra food path can absorb a loss — this is redundancy, and it is why connectance matters.

Analysis & questions

  1. What was your connectance, and how does it sit against the 0.05–0.3 range of published webs? If it is low, is that your habitat or your sampling effort — and how would you tell the difference?
  2. Which prediction were you most wrong about, and what did the web know that you didn't? (Look at how many food paths that species was carrying.)
  3. Your keystone candidate: state the case for calling it one, then the strongest case against. Paine's definition rests on impact being disproportionate to abundance — does your web contain the information needed to judge that, or would you need to go back and measure something else?
  4. Remove a producer instead of a predator. Why does the damage travel differently, and which direction does it travel?
  5. Your longest chain: is it 3, 4, or 5 levels? Use the ~10% energy-transfer figure to estimate how much of the producers' energy reaches your top predator, and say what that implies about how many of them the habitat can hold.

Take it further

Copy the CSV export — it is a food,eater edge list, the standard input format for network analysis — and open it in R with igraph or Python with networkx to compute degree distributions and centrality. A student who has built the web by hand first understands what those numbers are describing.
