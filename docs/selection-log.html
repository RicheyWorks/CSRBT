Method

What each number means, what it assumes, and the ways it is routinely misread.

What the entry layer records — and what it does not

The controls are the shared Field Entry Kit. Three choices on this page are about the data rather than the ergonomics.

Survival is two taps, not a box you type 0 or 1 into. When the fitness component is survival, each individual gets a two-way dial reading died / survived. Typing a bare 0 or 1 into a numeric box is a well-known source of silent error — a stray keystroke turns a death into a survival and nothing about the record looks wrong afterwards. Naming the two outcomes makes the mistake visible.

An unrecorded count is not a zero. When the component is a count, each individual gets a stepper that starts empty. Zero recruits is a real and important observation; not having checked yet is not, and averaging the two together would quietly bias every selection estimate on the page downward. The first tap starts at 1 rather than 0, because 0 is a value you should have to choose.

A trait value is typed, not stepped. A bill depth is a number you read off calipers, so it gets a plain field at full precision. Nothing is rounded on the way in — the repeatability analysis is computed from exactly what you recorded, and rounding at entry would inflate it.

Changing the fitness component does not reinterpret what you already recorded. A 1 entered as survived is not one recruited offspring. Switching the component keeps your values — losing data is worse — but says plainly that they were entered under a different definition, and offers to clear them. Silently carrying them over would put a fabricated fitness value into the differential, the intensity, the gradient and the predicted response, all at once and all invisibly.

Age class is a dial because it is ordinal, and left blank means left blank: an unrecorded age is not the same as an age you determined to be unknown, and both are available.

What this page is for

Long-term studies of marked individuals are how selection stopped being an inference and became a measurement. The design is always the same shape whatever the taxon: mark individuals so you can find them again, measure traits repeatedly and carefully enough to know your own error, record who survived and who bred, and link offspring to parents. Then selection is the difference between the population before an episode and the individuals who got through it, and heritability is the slope of offspring on parents. Everything on this page is that shape.

The measurement that carries the whole enterprise is not the fancy one. It is repeatability — whether you can measure the same animal twice and get the same number.

Repeatability

From a one-way ANOVA across individuals with repeated measures, R = s²among / (s²among + s²within) — the intraclass correlation. It is the proportion of the variance you measured that is real between-individual variation rather than your own hand.

Why it decides everything downstream. Random measurement error inflates the variance of a trait without adding any real covariance with fitness. The selection gradient is a regression slope with trait variance in its denominator, so error attenuates β toward zero, roughly by a factor of R. A gradient measured on a trait with R = 0.6 is on the order of 40% too shallow, and no amount of sample size fixes it — more animals measured badly gives a tighter confidence interval around the wrong number. This page reports R first, and prints the attenuation-corrected gradient beside the raw one so you can see the size of the problem.

R below about 0.7 means the trait as you are measuring it is mostly noise. The fix is method — a better landmark, a jig, a different instrument, two independent measurers — not statistics.

Differential, intensity, gradient

Selection differential S = mean of the survivors (or of breeders, weighted by fitness) minus the mean of everyone before the episode. It is the total change in the trait mean within a generation, and it is in the trait's own units.

Intensity i = S / SD, the differential in standard deviations. Use it to compare selection on bill depth against selection on body mass, or this year against last.

A note on divisors. S here is the fitness-weighted mean minus the mean, which is the population covariance of the trait with relative fitness. The standardization for i and β therefore uses the population SD (n divisor) as well. Mixing the two conventions — a population S against a sample SD — silently inflates β relative to i by n/(n−1) and breaks an identity that should hold exactly. The descriptive “SD before” tile is the ordinary sample SD and is labelled as such.

Gradient β is the slope from regressing relative fitness (w = W / mean W) on the standardized trait (z = (x − mean) / SD) — the Lande & Arnold (1983) formulation. In a multiple regression with several traits, β is direct selection on each trait with the others held constant.

S and β differ, and the difference is the point. S includes selection acting on every correlated trait you did not measure: if larger birds survive and bill depth correlates with body size, bill depth shows a differential even under no direct selection on bills at all. β with body size in the model removes that. This page computes univariate β only — one trait at a time — which is mathematically the same as the differential rescaled, and therefore carries exactly the same confound. To separate direct from indirect selection you need a multiple regression on all the correlated traits together, which is a desk job with the exported CSV, not a field readout. The page says so beside the number rather than letting a univariate β pass for a direct one.

Heritability, and the equation that keeps failing

Regressing offspring trait values on the mid-parent mean gives a slope that estimates h² directly. Against a single parent the slope estimates h²/2, and this page doubles it and says that it has.

The breeder's equation R = h²S predicts the change in the trait mean between generations. It is printed here, and it should be read with more suspicion than any other number on this page:

  • Parents and offspring usually share an environment as well as genes. Territory quality, maternal condition and food supply all inflate the parent–offspring slope, so a field h² is an upper bound on the genetic part unless you have cross-fostering or a pedigree model.
  • h² is a property of one population in one environment at one time, not of a trait. It changes when conditions change — often exactly when selection is strongest.
  • Predicted responses in wild populations frequently fail to appear. The usual culprits are environmental covariance between the trait and fitness, selection on an unmeasured correlated trait, and the trait's environmental sensitivity moving under the same conditions that caused the selection.

Treat R = h²S as a hypothesis the next generation will test, and record what actually happened, which is what a long-term study is for.

Episodes, and what counts as an answer

Selection is measured over a defined episode with a defined fitness component — survival through a drought, recruitment into the breeding population, matings obtained in one season. Different components can select in opposite directions on the same trait in the same year, and a figure quoted without its episode is uninterpretable. The episode field is on the Individuals tab and it goes into every export.

One episode is an anecdote about one year. Selection in the wild reverses: a run of dry years favours one extreme, a wet run favours the other, and the long-run mean can barely move while every single year shows strong selection. That is why the answer to "is this trait under selection" is a time series and not a p-value, and why the natural-history notes on each individual — what it was eating, where, in what condition — are the part of the record that eventually explains the pattern.

Sample size and honesty

This page flags small samples rather than hiding them: gradients on fewer than about 30 individuals, repeatability on fewer than about 10 individuals with repeats, and parent–offspring regressions on fewer than about 20 families are all reported with the n beside them and a note that the estimate is unstable. Nothing here computes a p-value, because the number that matters in selection studies is the effect size and its uncertainty, and because a significant β from a study that measured one trait badly is worse than no number at all.
