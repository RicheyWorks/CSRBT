One

What makes a plant carnivorous

The question is older than it looks, and the answer is a checklist rather than a vibe. A plant is carnivorous if it does all five of these. Drop any one and you are in the interesting territory at the edge, which is where several famous plants actually live.

StepWhat the plant must doWhere it is usually tested
1 AttractDraw prey to the trap — nectar, scent, colour, UV patterning, or a deceptive landing surface.Choice experiments; nectary anatomy.
2 CaptureRetain the prey once it arrives, by adhesive, by geometry, by a moving part, or by a surface it cannot walk back up.Direct observation. The easy one.
3 KillThe prey dies as a consequence of the trap.Direct observation.
4 DigestBreak the prey down — the plant's own enzymes, or a partner organism's.Enzyme assays; whether the plant makes its own protease.
5 AbsorbTake up the released nutrients, and gain a measurable benefit. 15N tracer work: how much of the plant's nitrogen came from prey.
Step 4 is where the arguments happen Roridula, from the South African Cape, catches insects on the stickiest leaves of any plant on Earth — and produces no digestive enzymes of its own. Its glands secrete resin, not mucilage, and enzymes and nutrients cannot diffuse through resin. What digests the prey is a resident assassin bug, Pameridea, which walks the sticky leaves unharmed, eats the catch, and defecates on the leaf. The plant absorbs the frass through gaps in an unusually thin cuticle, and gets about 70% of its nitrogen from prey that way — comparable to a sundew. So: it fails step 4 alone and passes it in partnership. Most authors now call it carnivorous by mutualism. Whether you agree is less interesting than knowing exactly which step is in dispute.

The same test sorts the other boundary cases. Triphyophyllum is carnivorous only during one juvenile phase, growing glandular leaves for a few weeks and then never again. Philcoxia holds sticky leaves underground, buried in white sand, and catches nematodes — confirmed by feeding it 15N-labelled worms. The bromeliads Brocchinia reducta and Catopsis berteroniana pass on attraction, capture and absorption but lean heavily on bacteria for digestion. Ibicella and Proboscidea are sticky and murderous and appear to absorb nothing at all — they fail step 5, which is the step that makes carnivory pay.

A plant that catches insects is not carnivorous. A plant that profits from catching them is.

Two

The five trap mechanisms

Carnivory has evolved independently at least a dozen times in the flowering plants, and it keeps arriving at the same five solutions. Learn the mechanism and the genus follows; learn the genus first and you will be memorising rather than reading.

Pitfall Sarracenia · Nepenthes · Darlingtonia · Heliamphora · Cephalotus · Brocchinia

A rolled leaf makes a container. Nectar on the rim brings prey to a surface that stops holding — wet peristome, loose wax crystals, downward hairs — and geometry does the rest. No moving parts, and by far the commonest solution.

Flypaper — adhesive Drosera · Pinguicula · Drosophyllum · Byblis · Philcoxia · (Roridula)

Stalked glands hold droplets of mucilage that are mostly water and behave like glue. In Drosera the tentacles and often the whole leaf then bend inward over minutes to hours; in Drosophyllum and Byblis nothing moves at all, and the glue alone does the work.

Snap trap Dionaea · Aldrovanda

Two lobes hinged on the midrib, fringed with interlocking spines, carrying trigger hairs on the inner face. Two touches within about thirty seconds and the lobes invert in a fraction of a second. Only two genera on Earth do this, and they are each other's closest relatives.

Suction — bladder Utricularia

The bladder pumps water out until its walls are under tension and the inside is below ambient pressure. A bristle is knocked, the door buckles inward, and water — carrying whatever was outside — is pulled in. The fastest movement known in the plant kingdom.

Lobster-pot — eel trap Genlisea · Sarracenia psittacina & S. minor · Darlingtonia (partly)

A tube lined with hairs all pointing one way. Entering is easy, reversing is not, and the prey walks itself to the digestive end. Genlisea runs its Y-shaped traps underground and takes protozoa rather than insects — a whole trap type built for prey you need a microscope to see.

Trap type is not a taxonomic rank. Sarracenia psittacina is a pitfall genus running a lobster pot; Darlingtonia uses downward hairs and translucent windows to do lobster-pot work inside a pitcher. The mechanisms are engineering solutions, and one plant may use two. Record the mechanism you actually observed, not the one the genus is famous for.

Three

Pitcher anatomy

A pitcher description will name these parts and assume you can find them. Most are visible on a plant on a windowsill; all of them are doing a job.

Peristome The collar around the mouth, usually ribbed and nectar-bearing. In Nepenthes it becomes frictionless when wet — the single most important capture surface on the plant.
Operculum / lid Held above the mouth. Keeps rain out in Sarracenia and Nepenthes; in S. purpurea it does not, and the pitcher deliberately fills with water.
Ala / wing The flange down the front seam of an upright pitcher. A walking surface that leads prey up to the mouth — and a character that separates Sarracenia species.
Areoles / fenestrations Translucent patches in a closed hood. A trapped insect flies at the light, hits chlorophyll-free tissue, and falls. Darlingtonia and S. minor both use them.
Tendril In Nepenthes the pitcher hangs from the tip of the leaf on a tendril — the blade above it is a photosynthetic petiole. Upper pitchers coil the tendril to climb.
Phyllodia Flat, trapless leaves. Sarracenia flava and S. oreophila make them late in the season; Cephalotus makes them alongside pitchers all year. Not a sign of ill health.
Waxy & retentive zones Below the mouth, a band of detachable wax crystals no insect foot can grip; below that, downward hairs or a digestive surface. Two different mechanisms, stacked.
Nectar spoon & drainage slit Heliamphora only. A tiny nectar-bearing spoon at the rear, and a slit part-way up the pitcher that fixes the water level — a plant that plumbed an overflow.

Four

Glands, tentacles and mucilage

Flypaper plants are described in gland language, and two distinctions carry most of the weight: whether a gland is stalked or sessile, and whether the leaf moves.

TermWhat it meansWhere you see it
Stalked gland
tentacle		 A gland raised on a stalk, tipped with a mucilage droplet. Captures. All of Drosera, Byblis, Drosophyllum; the long glands of Pinguicula.
Sessile gland Flush with the leaf surface, no stalk. Secretes enzymes and reabsorbs the digest. Pinguicula especially — the greasy feel of a butterwort leaf is the sessile layer.
Mucilage A polysaccharide gel that is mostly water. Sticky because it is viscoelastic, not because it cures.Everything above except Roridula, which secretes resin instead.
Snap tentacle A tentacle that flicks a captured insect toward the leaf centre in seconds rather than bending over minutes.Australian pygmy and some tuberous Drosera — D. glanduligera is the standard example.
Circinate vernation New leaves emerge coiled like a fern crozier and unroll. Many Drosera and Drosophyllum — but Drosophyllum uncoils outward, the opposite way, which is diagnostic.
Leaf-margin rolling The edge of the blade curls over a catch, bringing more sessile glands into contact. Pinguicula. Slow, subtle, and easy to miss unless you look along the leaf edge.
Wet does not mean healthy, and dry does not mean dead. Mucilage production drops in low humidity, in strong airflow, and in a plant that has just fed. A sundew with no visible dew has usually been moved rather than damaged, and reruns its glands within a few days of stable conditions. Record the conditions before you record the plant.

Why "don't poke the traps" is real advice and not folklore. A closure that catches nothing still costs the plant the full snap and 16 to 44 hours shut — and a trap that never reaches five signals never earns anything back. A trap only has a handful of cycles in it before it dies back. This is also why the number to record in a log is closures, not feedings: they are different events with different costs, and only one of them is nutrition.

What this card will not give you There is no table here of dormancy temperatures, photoperiods or durations by genus, and none in CP Bench either. Six

Work out the genus

Answer what you can see and skip what you cannot. Every answer narrows the list; skipping one just leaves it longer. This is a character key, not a dichotomous one — there is no wrong turn to get stuck down.

1 · What kind of trap?

2 · Where is it growing?

3 · Does the trap visibly move when it catches something?

4 · Where in the world — native range, not your greenhouse

The key answers "which genus", never "which species". Several of these genera hybridise freely — Sarracenia notoriously, Nepenthes nearly as much — and a plant bought from a nursery is more likely to be a hybrid than not. A genus you can defend beats a species you guessed, and CP Bench takes Sarracenia sp. as a perfectly good record.

Eight

The pairs that get confused

Six confusions worth being able to settle cold. Two of them are identification errors that cost nothing but embarrassment; four of them kill plants, and the cost is named on each card.

Drosophyllum  vs  Drosera

Both are covered in glistening stalked glands, and a photograph of the leaves alone will not separate them.

Separate them by

New leaves. Drosophyllum uncoils outward, away from the crown — every other circinate leaf in the group coils inward. And the tentacles never move.

What the mistake costs

The plant. Drosophyllum grows in dry alkaline Iberian scrub, resents root disturbance absolutely, and rots within weeks in the wet peat tray a sundew wants. Sow it where it will stay.

Temperate Pinguicula  vs  Mexican Pinguicula

Both are flat greasy rosettes for most of the year, and nurseries often sell them under the same care label.

Separate them by

What they do in autumn. Temperate species pull into a tight scaly hibernaculum and want to stay wet and cold. Mexicans switch to a succulent, gland-free winter rosette and want to go nearly dry.

What the mistake costs

The plant, either way. A wet Mexican in winter rots at the crown; a dried-out temperate hibernaculum never breaks.

Nepenthes lower  vs  upper pitcher

Not two plants — one plant. Lowers sit on the ground, squat and heavily winged; uppers hang from a climbing stem, funnel-shaped and often wingless, with the tendril coiled around a support.

Separate them by

Nothing: check the stem. If it is climbing, you are looking at uppers. Several species were named twice from the two forms before anyone grew one on.

What the mistake costs

An identification, and sometimes a purchase. Judge from lowers plus the leaf and stem, never from an upper pitcher photograph alone.

Sarracenia species  vs  the hybrid soup

All eight species cross freely, hybrids are fertile, and complex nursery crosses dominate the trade. A trumpet with a hooded top is not automatically S. flava × anything you can name.

Separate them by

Refusing to. Record the genus, photograph the pitcher, the lid from above, the flower and the whole plant, and let it be Sarracenia sp. or "unnamed hybrid" until a flower and a locality say otherwise.

What the mistake costs

Your data. A misapplied species name propagates into every record, label and seed packet that follows it, and is far harder to withdraw than it was to write.

Aldrovanda  vs  aquatic Utricularia

Both float rootlessly in the same still, acidic water, and both look at arm's length like a scruffy strand of pondweed.

Separate them by

Trap shape under a hand lens. Aldrovanda has whorled snap traps like tiny green clamshells; Utricularia has bladders, ovoid and stalked, often visibly dark with catch.

What the mistake costs

Potentially a legal problem. Aldrovanda is declining globally and protected in much of its range — worth knowing before you net a handful.

Carnivorous  vs  merely sticky

Ibicella, Proboscidea, many Silene and Plumbago, and a great many ordinary glandular-hairy plants catch insects on their stems and do nothing whatsoever with them.

Separate them by

The five-step test in section one. Sticky is step 2. Carnivory needs 4 and 5 as well, and those are the steps you cannot see by looking.

What the mistake costs

Nothing but a claim you cannot support — which is enough. "Glandular and insect-trapping" is an honest field note; "carnivorous" is a conclusion that needs enzyme or tracer work behind it.

Nine

Provenance, CITES and where the field stops

This is the group where the ethics are not an afterthought. Carnivorous plants are heavily poached, several are protected by international treaty, and their habitats are among the most reduced ecosystems on the continent. Read this section before you take anything.

Do not collect wild carnivorous plants. Longleaf pine savanna, the habitat of most North American Sarracenia, is estimated to have been reduced to a small fraction of its historical extent, and the remnant populations are small enough that individual plants matter. Everything worth growing is available nursery-propagated, from seed or tissue culture, at a price far below what a collected plant costs the population. Photograph it, log the locality, and report anything notable to the state Natural Heritage Program.

What CITES actually lists

Two levels, and the distinction matters if you ever move a plant across a border.

Listings are amended at the Conference of the Parties and national law is frequently stricter than the treaty. This table is a starting point for a conversation with your own authority, not a substitute for checking the current appendices before you ship anything.

What to record when you do collect — seed, or a nursery plant

  • Where it came from, by name: the nursery, the seed bank, the friend, the society seed exchange with its lot number. "Bought online" is not provenance.
  • Whether it is wild-collected, ex-situ propagated, or of unknown origin — and say unknown when it is unknown, because the alternative is inventing a lineage.
  • The name as you received it, verbatim, including a cultivar name in single quotes and any 'cf.' or 'aff.' the seller used. Then your own determination, separately, dated.
  • The date acquired, which becomes the anchor for every growth record afterwards.
  • A photograph on arrival, before your conditions have changed anything.
