A field mode for the harness

Douglas Explorer Mode

Point the kit at a real place. Enter everything the place actually shows you, invent nothing, and write down by name each thing the harness could not hold.

Most of this kit's experiments are simulations: a seeded stream, a known answer, an oracle to check it against. Douglas Explorer Mode is the other direction. You stand somewhere real, you record what is in front of you, and the instruments meet data that did not come from a random number generator and does not care whether they are ready for it.

The mode is named for David Douglas, who did exactly this for eleven years with a notebook, a plant press, and no way of knowing whether any of it would arrive home.

The patron

Born 25 June 1799 at Scone, Perthshire, son of a stonemason. Apprenticed at eleven into the gardens at Scone Palace, seven years under the head gardener, then Valleyfield and the Glasgow Botanic Garden, where William Jackson Hooker took him to lectures and then recommended him to the Royal Horticultural Society. That recommendation is the whole hinge of his life: a gardener's boy with no standing became the Society's collector in North America.

Three expeditions. Eastern North America in 1823. The Pacific Northwest from July 1824 to October 1827 — the great one. A third from 1829, through the Columbia, down to San Francisco in 1832, and on to Hawaii in January 1834.

He sent home roughly 240 species new to Britain. Walk through almost any British or Irish plantation and you are walking through his shipping manifest: Douglas fir, Sitka spruce, sugar pine, western white pine, ponderosa, lodgepole, Monterey pine, grand fir, noble fir; and in the borders, flowering currant, salal, lupin, penstemon, California poppy. Over eighty plants and animals carry douglasii.

He was probably the first European mountaineer in North America — Mount Brown in the spring of 1826 — and the second European on the summit of Mauna Loa.

The mode

The observation this mode was built to record. It is the kit's first piece of image evidence, and how it is stored is a claim the kit has to be able to prove — so the chain from the bytes that arrived to the bytes you are looking at is checked by tools/verify/verify_evidence.py, not asserted here.

The plate

Douglas Explorer Mode is a discipline, not a feature. Six rules, all of them his:

  • Record the place, not the idea of the place. What is in the frame goes in. What you expect to be there does not.
  • Uncertain identification is data; a confident guess is not. If the tree could be Calocedrus or a young fir, it is entered as uncertain, at the rank you can actually defend.
  • Save the specimens before the provisions. When something has to be dropped, drop your own convenience, never the record.
  • Name what you could not carry. Every observation the harness had no field for is written down as a gap, in the record, where it will be found.
  • Write the hypotheses before the numbers. The engine grades them either way; the only thing at stake is whether you learn anything.
  • The instrument is a suspect too. A clean result from a tool that examined nothing is the failure this kit keeps finding. Ask what it looked at.

Six gaps from one photograph. That is the finding, and it is a better result than a clean run would have been: the harness takes a simulation's worth of information and a real place has more. Every one of these is a field the grammar could grow — a site: line, a cover: quantity distinct from a count, a confidence rank, an evidence reference — and none of them can be designed honestly without a real observation to design against. Now there is one.

Lab report — a west-shore montane stand and the lake below it

Abstract. A single photograph of a mixed-conifer stand above Lake Tahoe is worked up as far as the evidence in it will honestly go. Six canopy elements, five understory elements and four structural features were recorded and run through the kit's diversity, beta-diversity and island-biogeography instruments. The stand reads as a two-species-dominated upper montane forest (J′ = 0.74) beneath a very even shrub-and-graminoid gap flora (J′ = 0.96), with canopy and understory sharing no taxa at all. The central methodological result is negative and is the point of the exercise: at this resolution almost nothing is identifiable to species, and the report is structured so that the rank at which each identification actually stops is visible rather than smoothed over.

2. Site and physiography

Lake Tahoe sits in a graben — a fault-bounded block that dropped between two rising ranges, the Sierra Nevada crest to the west and the Carson Range to the east. The basin was then dammed at its north end by andesitic flows from the Mount Pluto volcanic centre and reworked by Pleistocene glaciers. The bedrock under the stand and under the far skyline is overwhelmingly granodiorite of the Cretaceous Sierra Nevada batholith, with Miocene–Pliocene volcanics capping parts of the northern rim. That parent material matters ecologically: granodiorite weathers to coarse, sandy, nutrient-poor and sharply drained soil, which is a large part of why Jeffrey pine holds the drier slopes here and why the understory is dominated by sclerophyll shrubs rather than herbs.

The range on the skyline. The plate shows the basin's opposite rim, a long ridge carrying snow only on its highest north-facing benches. From a west-shore viewpoint that is the Carson Range; from an east-shore viewpoint it would be the Sierra crest. unresolved The photograph does not settle which, and neither does the file — it carries no GPS. The site is recorded as "Lake Tahoe, west shore" on the observer's testimony, and the specific range identification is left open rather than guessed. Resolving it needs one datum the harness currently has no field for: a bearing, or a coordinate.

Snow surviving only on high north aspects while the viewpoint is bare, combined with fully expanded shrub foliage, places the observation in early summer inf. The lake surface stands at 6,225 ft; the viewpoint is above it on a slope bench, which puts the stand in the upper montane belt.

3. The lake

What the plate shows of the water is a deep, saturated blue running unbroken to the far shore, with no green cast and no visible turbidity plume obs. That colour is a reading, not a decoration: it is what an ultraoligotrophic water column looks like from shore. With very little suspended particulate and very little algal biomass, short wavelengths scatter back out of a great depth of water while the reds are absorbed, and the lake returns blue. Tahoe is 1,645 ft deep — the second-deepest lake in the United States — and holds roughly 39 trillion gallons, so there is a great deal of depth doing that absorbing.

What is in the water reg — none of this is visible in the plate, and it is recorded as context:

CompartmentComposition

ZooplanktonStructurally rearranged. Mysis relicta was introduced in the mid-1960s to feed trout; instead it ate the large cladocerans, and Daphnia and Bosmina collapsed. Removing the grazers that clear algal cells is a clarity story as much as a food-web one.

Invertebrate invadersAsian clam, signal crayfish; Eurasian watermilfoil and curlyleaf pondweed in the shallows.

Clarity is the basin's index of condition and it is measured, not estimated: a Secchi disk lowered until it disappears. The 2025 annual average was 69.2 ft, up from 62.3 ft in 2024 but not statistically different from recent years, against a 1967–71 baseline of 97.4 ft that remains the restoration target. Fine sediment from roads and disturbed ground, algal growth, and invasive species are the three drivers. The forest in this plate is directly implicated in the first of them: the slope's vegetation and litter layer are what stop granitic fines reaching the lake.

The assemblage — a yellow pine plus true fir canopy over a sclerophyll shrub gap flora on granitic soil at this elevation — is Sierra Nevada upper montane mixed-conifer forest, in its Jeffrey pine–white fir expression inf. That call rests on physiognomy and setting, which are legitimate community-level evidence, and does not depend on any of the uncertain species rows above.

5. What lives in those trees

Nothing animal is visible in the plate obs. What follows is the fauna this structure supports, entered as regional context reg — and the structure is the point, because the plate does record the habitat features these species require.

Structure in the plateWhat it supports
Closed conifer canopy Douglas squirrel — the chickaree, the animal that cuts and caches most of the cone crop — plus mountain chickadee, red-breasted nuthatch, golden-crowned kinglet, brown creeper on the trunks, Steller's jay, western tanager, and olive-sided flycatcher calling from the high dead tips.
Large-seeded pines Clark's nutcracker, which caches pine seed by the thousand and is a principal dispersal agent; black bear and mule deer through the stand seasonally.
The snag The richest single feature in frame. Woodpeckers — white-headed, hairy, northern flicker, pileated — excavate it; everything that cannot excavate then uses the holes: mountain bluebird, nuthatches, northern flying squirrel, and roosting bats. Standing dead wood is a limiting resource in managed forest, and one snag in a photograph is a real record.
Dying and stressed stems The agents that make snags: Jeffrey pine beetle Dendroctonus jeffreyi, fir engraver Scolytus ventralis, mountain pine beetle D. ponderosae; and dwarf mistletoe Arceuthobium abietinum in the firs.
Bark surfaces Wolf lichen Letharia vulpina — the chartreuse crust on Sierra conifer bark — with beard lichens in the wetter crowns. Epiphytes are the layer most often left out of a stand description and the one most sensitive to air quality.
Canopy gap and shrub layer The browse and cover layer: mule deer, dusky grouse, fox sparrow, green-tailed towhee, and the small-mammal community the forest's raptors hunt.

6. Quantitative results

Run through the kit's instruments from docs/tahoe-westshore.eco. The canopy is moderately even at J′ = 0.74 with two elements carrying most of the cover; the gap flora is almost perfectly even at J′ = 0.96. Bray–Curtis between canopy and understory is 1.00 — complete dissimilarity, because they share no taxon at all. That is not a subtle result and it should not be dressed up as one: it is what vertical stratification looks like when you record two layers as separate communities, and it confirms the layers were scored independently rather than blurred.

The island model was given the lake as the barrier: colonisation reduced by 15 km of open water, extinction raised by the small effective area of a slope bench. It is the right shape of question for this landscape — the two rims of a graben are a natural pair of habitat islands with a 40-kilometre water gap — but its parameters here are illustrative, not fitted, and the report claims nothing from its output.

7. Limitations

  • One photograph, one moment, no plot. There is no quadrat, no transect and no repeat visit, so nothing here is a density, a basal area, or a trend.
  • Cover was estimated by eye from an image, and entered in a slot the engine calls a count. Proportions survive that; Chao1 and the rare-species estimators do not, because they assume individuals.
  • Occlusion. Only the canopy facing the camera is scored. Anything behind the first row of crowns is absent from the record for reasons that have nothing to do with the stand.
  • No ground truth. Not one identification was checked against a specimen, a key, or a second observer.

What would raise the confidence tier is unglamorous and specific: a bearing and a coordinate; two or three photographs at 1–2 m of bark, foliage and litter; one cone; and a return visit. None of that is a software problem.

10. The seam this experiment actually found

The first pass of this report listed six things the harness could not hold: coordinates, elevation, aspect and slope; date and observer; the source photograph; percent cover as distinct from a count; identification confidence; and abiotic variables beyond four. That list was correct about the .eco protocol grammar and wrong about the kit, and the correction is the most useful result of the whole exercise.

Every one of those fields already exists in this kit's own field pages:

Said to be missingWhere it already lives
Coordinates, slope, aspectreleve.html — latitude, longitude, slope and aspect are recorded fields, and the export carries coordinateUncertaintyInMeters with a rule that an unknown coordinate stays empty rather than becoming a zero that would read as perfect precision
Percent cover as its own quantityreleve.html — Braun-Blanquet and Daubenmire cover classes with class midpoints, which is exactly the right instrument and is what section 4 should have used
Date and observerAll three export pages — eventDate, recordedBy
Whether a specimen was keptcollection-sheet.html — basisOfRecord, the one place a field decision changes a controlled value
Stand structure: DBH, basal area, snagsstand-sheet.html

So the harness is not missing these concepts. It has two halves that do not speak to each other. The HTML field pages record an observation properly and emit Darwin Core — the same standard GBIF and iNaturalist use, which is why every source in section 9.1 says "direct". The .eco protocol engine holds the analysis — diversity, beta diversity, life tables, island biogeography, graded hypotheses, the export bundle — and cannot read a word of what the field pages produce.

This observation went in through the wrong half. It should have been entered on releve.html as cover classes with a located, dated header, and on stand-sheet.html for the snag, and the protocol engine should have consumed that export. That is one seam, and closing it is worth more than the six separate directives the first pass proposed:

the plugin to build:  Darwin Core / relevé export  →  .eco protocol ingest

  releve.html      →  cover classes, located and dated  →  data: with a cover type
  stand-sheet.html →  DBH, basal area, snags           →  structure, life table
  collection-sheet →  vouchered records, basisOfRecord  →  identification confidence
  GBIF / iNat / CCH2 / MyCoPortal  →  the same Darwin Core  →  the same ingest

One reader, and the kit's own field pages and half the world's biodiversity infrastructure arrive through the same door. That is the finding. It took one photograph of a hillside to surface it, which is the argument for Douglas Explorer Mode.

Queued — what this experiment could not finish

This was the kit's first attempt at a real ecological experiment, and the honest outcome is that it ran partway. The queue is now headed by the seam in section 10 — a Darwin Core reader — which subsumes most of what follows. Held, with the reason, rather than half-built:

QueuedBlocked byNeeded to unblock
site: directive — coordinates, elevation, aspect, slope, bearing Grammar change in ExperimentSpec.java A JDK 17 build. Designed against a real observation now, which is why it was worth waiting.
cover: as a quantity distinct from a count Same, plus a decision on which estimators may consume it Chao1 and rarefaction must refuse cover data rather than silently accept it.
evidence: directive linking a record to a hash in the evidence store Same The store and its verifier exist and pass; only the protocol link is missing.
Identification confidence as a field Same Every row in section 4 is currently prose because there is nowhere to put a rank.
Running the protocol itself The engine needs JDK 17 and log4j; this was assembled from a machine with neither ./gradlew ecologyExperiment -Pspec=docs/tahoe-westshore.eco on the host.

The evidence pipeline, by contrast, is finished and proven: fifteen checks covering storage, content-addressing, self-description and publication, including that the copy embedded in this page is byte-identical to the file on disk.
