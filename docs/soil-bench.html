The temperature standards, and what they are for

Two published thresholds are in wide use and they agree with each other. Both exist to kill pathogens and weed seeds, which is a different goal from making good compost — a pile can be excellent compost without ever meeting them, and meeting them does not make the compost finished.

methodrequirementsource
In-vessel or static aerated pile 55 °C (131 °F) or higher for 3 days 40 CFR 503 App. B (PFRP); USDA NOP states 131–170 °F for 3 days
Windrow 55 °C or higher for 15 days, with at least 5 turnings in that period 40 CFR 503 App. B (PFRP); USDA NOP concurs

The upper bound matters too. The thermophilic organisms doing the work operate to roughly 65–66 °C; above that they begin to die off and the pile cooks itself into a stall. NOP caps the acceptable range at 170 °F (77 °C) for the pathogen requirement, but a pile running at 70 °C is not a better pile, it is one that needs turning and probably water.

This page does not certify anything. It counts the days and turnings you logged and compares them against the numbers above. Whether a given batch meets a regulatory standard depends on probe placement, how many probes, where in the pile, record-keeping practice and the rules of your certifier or jurisdiction. Use this to run the pile well and to see when you are off track; use your certifier's own protocol for anything that has to be defended.

Why the C:N calculation uses mass and not buckets

The ratio that matters to the organisms is carbon atoms to nitrogen atoms in the dry material. Volume is a poor proxy because bulk density varies roughly eight-fold across common feedstocks — straw is around 60 kg/m³ and fresh manure around 600 — and because wet material is mostly water. So each ingredient is converted:

wet mass = volume × bulk density
dry mass = wet mass × (1 − moisture)
N = dry mass × %N  ·  C = N × (C:N)
blend C:N = ΣC ÷ ΣN

Note the last line: the blend ratio is the total carbon over the total nitrogen, not the average of the individual ratios. Averaging the ratios is the common mistake and it gives a badly wrong answer whenever the ingredients differ in nitrogen content, which is always.

The 25:1 to 40:1 target is the USDA National Organic Program's initial-ratio requirement, and it lines up with the range most composting guidance gives. Below about 20:1 the surplus nitrogen leaves as ammonia — that is what a pile smelling of ammonia is telling you. Above about 40:1 the organisms are nitrogen-limited and the pile sits cold.

The feedstock numbers are typical values, and they vary enormously. Published C:N for "leaves" spans roughly 40 to 90 depending on species and how long they have lain; manure depends on bedding, diet and age; food scraps depend entirely on what was in them. Every one of the four fields is editable per ingredient for that reason. Treat the output as an order-of-magnitude guide that tells you which way to adjust, not a specification.

The moisture squeeze test

Composting runs on water films. The field test is to squeeze a handful hard: nothing at all means too dry, one or two drops is right, a stream means too wet. Conventionally that corresponds to roughly 50–60% moisture by mass, but the squeeze is the practical instrument and a moisture meter in a heterogeneous pile mostly measures where you put it. Too dry stalls the pile; too wet drives out air, the pile goes anaerobic, and the smell changes from earthy to sour.

The mix indices are ordinal, and only comparative

Each component carries four scores from 0 to 5 for water holding, aeration, cation exchange and nutrient content. These are ranks for comparing components against one another, not measurements: a water score of 4 does not mean four of anything, and the weighted average the page shows is an aid to reasoning, not a predicted water-holding capacity.

A mix is judged by drenching it, letting it drain, and seeing how it behaves — how fast water passes, how much stays, how it re-wets after drying out, and whether it slumps in the pot after a season. No index predicts the last of those, and it is often the one that decides whether a mix was any good.

The nutrient score exists for one specific reason: growers of nutrient-intolerant plants — carnivorous plants above all — need mixes whose components score zero. The page flags a mix that is not nutrient-free so that requirement is visible rather than remembered.

Texture by feel

The key is the standard ribbon-and-grit procedure used by soil scientists and extension services, and with practice it is reliable to within one class of a laboratory particle-size analysis. Two honest limits. It takes calibration: work through a few samples of known texture before trusting your hand. And texture is only half the story — structure, the way particles aggregate, decides how a soil actually drains and roots, and two soils of identical texture can behave completely differently depending on organic matter and how they have been worked.

Texture cannot be changed by any practical amendment. Adding sand to a clay soil in anything less than overwhelming quantity makes it worse, not better. Structure can be changed, and organic matter is how.

What this page deliberately does not do

It does not estimate microbial biomass, fungal-to-bacterial ratio, or "soil life" from anything you can enter here. Those need respiration measurement, direct counts, or sequencing, and the numbers that circulate for them in gardening sources are frequently invented. It does not convert a compost temperature curve into a maturity or stability rating, which needs a respirometry test. And it does not give a pH or nutrient recommendation, because that needs a lab test on your soil and a target crop.
