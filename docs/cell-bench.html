Method

What the entry layer records — and what it does not

The controls are the shared Field Entry Kit, and this page is the one that made it grow a new component.

Two kinds of number live on a bench, and they want different controls. A count of squares, a dilution, a volume, a path length — you set those, so they get a stepper you can hold to run. An absorbance, a stock density, an extinction coefficient — you read those off an instrument to three or six significant figures, and a stepper is the wrong control entirely: you would tap two thousand times to reach 1.842. Those get a plain typed entry at the same size as everything else, asking for the decimal keypad and offering no arrows to pretend the value is scrollable. Nothing is rounded on the way in.

Blank still means blank. Elapsed time, confluency at harvest and cell cycle length start unrecorded — the box is dashed and the slider says so in words rather than parking at zero. A confluency of 0% is a real observation and an unrecorded confluency is not, and the two must not collapse into each other. Tap once on an empty cycle-length stepper and it starts at a plausible 24 h rather than at zero, because zero is never the value you meant.

The mitotic index computes without a cycle length. Supply one and the phase durations follow; leave it blank and you still get the index, without the page inventing a cycle to multiply by.

Counting is a Poisson process, and that sets your precision

Cells land in a haemocytometer square at random. The count in a square is therefore Poisson, and the coefficient of variation of a total count of N cells is CV = 1/√N. That single fact governs the whole exercise:

  • Count 100 cells → CV = 10%. Your "1.0 × 10⁶ cells/mL" is 1.0 ± 0.1.
  • Count 400 cells → CV = 5%.
  • Count 25 cells → CV = 20%, and the number is nearly useless for seeding a quantitative assay.

This is why the convention is roughly 20–50 cells per large square across four squares. Below that you have not counted enough to know the density; above it, cells overlap and you systematically undercount, which no amount of arithmetic recovers. The page reports CV beside every count and says which side of the range you are on.

cells/mL = (cells counted / squares counted) × chamber factor × dilution factor

The chamber factor is 10⁴ for a standard Neubauer at 0.1 mm depth, because one large square holds 0.1 µL. If your chamber differs, so does the factor — check it rather than assuming.

Viability

Trypan blue is a membrane-integrity dye, not a viability assay. It reports which cells have holes in them right now. A cell that is committed to apoptosis but still membrane-intact counts as live; a cell damaged by your pipetting counts as dead. Exposure time matters too — leave cells in trypan blue for more than a few minutes and viability drops because the dye is toxic, so the number you get partly measures how long you took.

Below about 80–90% viability the culture is usually not in a state worth starting an experiment from. That threshold is a working convention, not a rule: what matters is whether it is normal for your line and your protocol.

Doubling time

PD = log₂(N_f/N_i) is the number of population doublings; dividing elapsed time by it gives the doubling time. The figure is only meaningful inside exponential growth. Cells that spent part of the interval in lag phase, or that hit confluency and stopped, give a doubling time that is an average over states the population was never actually in, and it will be too long. The page flags confluency above 80% for this reason.

Population doubling is also not the same as cell division: it is a net figure, so death and detachment inflate it. If you need division rate rather than net expansion, you need a label.

Standard curves

The page fits a straight line by least squares and reports slope, intercept and r². A high r² does not validate a curve. It says the points you chose sit near a line; it says nothing about whether the assay is linear where your unknown falls, whether the blank was right, or whether the curve applies at all outside the range you measured.

Extrapolation is the common failure. An unknown whose absorbance sits above the top standard is not "off the top of the curve, so roughly this much" — it is unmeasured. Dilute it and read it again. The page refuses to quietly extrapolate and says so instead.

Beer–Lambert holds only where absorbance is roughly linear in concentration — conventionally below about A = 1.0, above which stray light and detector response bend the relationship. Read a high absorbance as a signal to dilute.

A260/A280

Approximately 1.8 for DNA and 2.0 for RNA are conventional targets, and a low ratio conventionally suggests protein or phenol carry-over. A260/A230 below about 2.0 conventionally suggests guanidine, phenol or carbohydrate. Treat all of these as rules of thumb: the ratios are pH- and buffer-sensitive, they cannot distinguish DNA from RNA from free nucleotides, and a beautiful ratio on a degraded sample is still a degraded sample. Purity ratios are a screen. Integrity needs a gel or a bioanalyser.

Mitotic index

MI = (cells in division / total cells) × 100. The onward step — multiplying the proportion of cells in a phase by the cell-cycle length to get the duration of that phase — is the standard teaching exercise, and it rests on assumptions worth stating out loud:

  • The population is asynchronous and in steady exponential growth. A synchronised or arrested population breaks the inference completely.
  • The cycle length is known independently. You cannot get it from this count.
  • Every phase is equally easy to see. Prophase and telophase are routinely under-scored because they are harder to recognise, which shortens their apparent duration.
  • Fixation captured the population as it was, without selectively losing mitotic cells — rounded mitotic cells detach more readily in culture.

Because of the last two, phase durations from this method should be read as approximate and systematically biased against the short, hard-to-see phases. That is a fine thing to say in a lab report, and a much better answer than a table of times quoted to three figures.
