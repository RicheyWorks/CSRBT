Ecology Glossary

Every term the science kit uses, in plain words — the vocabulary for the labs, the workbench readouts, and the field guide. Attributions name the originator where a term has one.

Community & diversity

Abundance
How many individuals of a species there are — or, in the kit's mapping, how many times a key is touched. The distribution of abundances is what most diversity metrics read.
Richness (S)
The simplest diversity number: how many different species (or categories) are present at all.
Shannon index (H′) Shannon 1948
Diversity as information: high when there are many species and their abundances are even. Rises with both richness and evenness.
Evenness (J′) Pielou 1966
How equally the individuals are shared out, from 0 (one species dominates) to 1 (perfectly even). H′ divided by its maximum possible value.
Simpson index Simpson 1949
The chance two randomly-drawn individuals are the same species — a dominance reading; less sensitive to rare species than Shannon.
Hill number (N₁) Hill 1973
Diversity converted into an effective species count: "100 species present, but the community behaves like 11 equally-common ones." The friendliest single figure.
Chao1
An estimate of true richness including species your survey likely missed, computed from how many species you saw only once or twice.
Rank–abundance curve
Species plotted from most to least abundant. Its shape is diagnostic: the geometric series suggests niche preemption; MacArthur's broken stick suggests even sharing.
Rarefaction
The fair way to compare richness between surveys of different effort: how many species would you have seen at the same sample size?

Comparing communities — β diversity

Jaccard index
Of all species found in either of two sites, the fraction found in both. Presence-only; abundances ignored.
Sørensen index
Like Jaccard but weights shared species more heavily. Also presence-only.
Bray–Curtis dissimilarity
Difference between two communities counting abundances, 0 (identical) to 1 (nothing shared). The kit's shift detector reads consecutive windows with it.
Renkonen index
A proportion-based similarity that stays fair when two samples have very different sizes — the fix for the classic unequal-effort mistake.
Turnover
How much a community's membership changes between two looks — between windows, generations, or survey intervals.

Populations & demography

Life table Deevey 1947
A cohort's survival bookkeeping: of those born, how many are still alive at each age. Summarized by survivorship type.
Survivorship types I / II / III
I: live long, die together (humans, batch purges). II: constant risk at every age (songbirds). III: most die young, survivors persist (oysters, fast-evicting caches).
Logistic growth Verhulst 1838
Growth that starts fast and levels off at a ceiling: rate r, carrying capacity K.
Carrying capacity (K)
The population ceiling an environment supports — the plateau of the logistic curve, or a cache's capacity.
Lincoln–Petersen estimate
Population size from mark–recapture: N̂ ≈ (marked × second catch) ÷ recaptured. Assumes a closed population, kept marks, even mixing.
Euler–Lotka equation
Combines age-specific survival (lx) and reproduction (mx) into the population's net reproductive rate R₀ and intrinsic growth rate r.

Space & islands

Quadrat
A fixed-area frame you drop and count inside — field ecology's oldest quantitative method. Counts per quadrat reveal spatial pattern.
Transect
A line laid across a habitat, sampled at intervals — the standard way to place quadrats objectively.
Index of dispersion
Variance ÷ mean of quadrat counts: ≈1 random, <1 regular (even spacing), >1 clumped (patches).
Morisita index Morisita 1959
The same random/regular/clumped reading, robust to how many individuals you counted.
Island biogeography MacArthur & Wilson 1967
Species number on an island as a dynamic equilibrium: immigration falls as the island fills, extinction rises, and richness settles where the curves cross — while composition keeps churning.
Metapopulation Levins 1969
A population of populations: habitat patches winking out (local extinction) and being recolonized from neighbors or a mainland. Equilibrium occupancy p* = 1 − e/c.

Food webs

Food web
The whole set of feeding links in a community — who eats whom. Drawn as arrows that point from the food to the eater, i.e. in the direction the energy travels. Build one by tapping in the Food Web Builder.
Trophic level
How many feeding steps a species sits above the producers. Producers are level 1; a consumer's level is one more than its highest-level food. Fractional levels are used in the literature when a species eats at several levels (omnivory) — the builder rounds to the longest path, which is the teaching convention.
Producer / consumer
A producer makes its own food (plants, algae — the base of every web); a consumer eats something else. A consumer with no incoming arrow has no food source and cannot persist — the builder flags it.
Food-chain length
The longest path from a producer to a top predator. Real chains are short — usually 3–5 levels — because roughly 90% of the energy is lost at each transfer, so there is little left to support a sixth.
Connectance L/S²
Realized feeding links divided by all possible ones. Published webs mostly fall between ≈0.05 and ≈0.3; much lower usually means an under-sampled web, much higher means links that aren't really there.
Keystone species Paine 1969
A species whose effect on the community is large out of proportion to its abundance — remove it and the web restructures. Paine found it by physically removing sea stars from tidal plots; the builder's knockout test is the same experiment, done cheaply.
Secondary extinction
A species lost because another was lost — it had no remaining food path. Counting them after a knockout is the standard robustness measure for a web.
Trophic cascade
The knock-on effect of changing one level that ripples down the others — wolves → elk → willows is the famous case. A cascade is about abundances shifting; a secondary extinction is the harder version, where the species goes to zero.

Botany & vegetation

Relevé Braun-Blanquet 1932
A vegetation plot record: every species present, with its cover, by layer. The unit of phytosociology and still the commonest way to describe a stand. Record it in the Relevé.
Cover
The proportion of ground a species' foliage would shade if projected straight down. Because layers overlap, cover summed across strata routinely exceeds 100% — that is correct, not an error.
Cover class
An ordinal band (Braun-Blanquet r,+,1–5; Daubenmire 1–6) standing in for a percentage. Averaging class numbers is meaningless; convert to class midpoints first, and remember a midpoint is not a measurement.
Stratum
A vegetation layer defined by height — overstory, subcanopy, tall and low shrub, herb, bryophyte. Recording by stratum is what lets you say a species is present but suppressed rather than simply present.
Line-point intercept
Cover measured by counting: drop a pin at fixed intervals along a tape and record what it touches. Only the first hit is top canopy. Far more repeatable between observers than ocular estimation, and slower.
Coefficient of conservatism C-value
A 0–10 score of how faithful a native species is to intact, undegraded habitat, assigned by regional expert panel — not computable from data and not transferable between regions.
Floristic Quality Index Swink & Wilhelm 1979
Mean C × √N. A screening measure of site condition. Rises with richness, so it is sensitive to plot size and effort — never compare an FQI across different plot sizes.
Wetland indicator status
OBL, FACW, FAC, FACU, UPL — how reliably a species occurs in wetlands, regionally assigned and periodically revised. The cover-weighted prevalence index (≤3.0) is one of the three wetland criteria; hydric soil and hydrology are the others.
Phenophase
The stage of the annual cycle a plant is in — vegetative, budding, flowering, fruiting, dispersing, senescent. Determines whether a specimen is keyable, and whether two visits are comparable.
Voucher
A pressed, labelled specimen deposited so the identification behind a record can be checked later. An unvouchered record is a memory. Sterile material often cannot be keyed at all.
Growth form
Tree, shrub, forb, graminoid, fern, vine, bryophyte. The functional spectrum often compares better between distant sites than the species list, because different floras converge on the same forms.
Ligule
The small flap or fringe where a grass blade meets its sheath. Its shape and size key more grasses than any other single character — and its absence, with a triangular stem, says sedge.
Ocrea
The papery sheath wrapping the stem at the node in many Polygonaceae. One of the fastest family diagnostics in the flora.
Inflorescence
The arrangement of flowers on a plant — raceme, spike, panicle, umbel, head, cyme. The developmental order differs: a raceme opens bottom-up, a cyme opens from the centre out.
Actinomorphic / zygomorphic
Radially versus bilaterally symmetric flowers. Bilateral symmetry is nearly always a pollinator-specialisation signal.
Superior / inferior ovary
Whether the other floral parts attach below the ovary or above it. One of the hardest-working characters in any key — look for a swelling beneath the petals.
Axillary bud
The bud in the angle where a leaf meets the stem. It is the test for simple versus compound: a bud sits at the base of a true leaf, never at the base of a leaflet.
Clonal / genet vs ramet
A genet is one genetic individual; a ramet is one stem of it. An aspen stand or a Gambel oak thicket may be a single genet with thousands of ramets, which makes "how many individuals" a much harder question than it looks.

Fungi & mycology

Sporocarp / fruit body
The spore-producing structure — the mushroom, bracket or truffle. It is the reproductive organ of a mycelium that may be decades old and hectares wide, which is why counting fruit bodies is a proxy for abundance and not a measure of it. Record them in the Collection Sheet.
Mycelium
The fungus itself: the network of hyphae threading soil, wood or litter. Almost everything a fungal survey never sees.
Guild
How a fungus gets its carbon — ectomycorrhizal, saprotrophic (on wood or on litter), parasitic, mycoparasitic, or lichenised. In the field it is assigned at genus level, and a fair number of genera contain species in more than one guild.
Ectomycorrhizal (ECM)
A mutualism in which the fungus sheathes a host tree's fine roots, trading soil nitrogen and phosphorus for host carbon. ECM fungi fruit near a compatible host, which is why host tree is half the identification.
Saprotroph
A decomposer of dead material. Worth splitting into wood and litter saprotrophs, because the two respond to different moisture and different substrate supply.
Hymenophore
The spore-bearing surface: gills, pores, teeth, ridges, a smooth face, or an enclosed gleba. The first branch of nearly every macrofungal key.
Spore print
The deposit left by a cap set gills-down on paper for several hours. The hardest macroscopic character an agaric has, and the one most often skipped. When the print contradicts the key result, the print wins.
Universal veil
Tissue enclosing the whole young fruit body; it leaves a volva, a rim, or warts on the cap. Frequently buried below the litter line — which is why a collection taken with a knife instead of dug can be missing its most important character.
Partial veil
Tissue running from cap margin to stipe; it leaves a ring, a ring zone, or a cobwebby cortina. A different structure from the universal veil, with different diagnostic weight.
Substrate
What the fruit body is actually emerging from. A fruit body arising from buried wood looks terrestrial and is not — a distinction that changes the guild.
Macrochemical spot test
A reagent (KOH, ammonia, iron salts, Melzer's, guaiac, phenol) applied to cuticle or flesh. Separates species within a genus; almost never separates genera. A test not run and a test that gave no reaction are different results.
Amyloid / dextrinoid
The reaction in Melzer's reagent: amyloid reads blue-black, dextrinoid red-brown, inamyloid nothing. Chiefly read on spores under a scope.
Chao1 Chao 1984
A lower-bound richness estimator built from rare species: with F1 singletons and F2 doubletons, Chao1 = S_obs + F1(F1−1)/(2(F2+1)). Central to fungal surveys because a fruiting survey undercounts badly — a fungus that did not fruit that day cannot be counted. It says "at least this many", never "this many".
Exsiccata / fungarium specimen
A dried voucher. Dried at roughly 40–45 °C with airflow: hotter degrades DNA and cooks the tissue, cooler lets a fleshy cap spoil before it dries.
Rhizomorph
A cord of aggregated hyphae that transports water and nutrients through soil or under bark. The black shoestrings of Armillaria are the textbook case, and a field character in their own right.
Hypogeous
Fruiting below ground — truffles and false truffles. Invisible to a walking survey, which is one reason such surveys undercount ectomycorrhizal richness.
cf. / aff.
Written before a species epithet: cf. means "compare with — probably this, not confirmed"; aff. means "close to this but likely distinct". The honest way to label a collection that needs a microscope.

Genetics

Punnett square
The grid of all gamete combinations from two parents — the prediction a cross's counts are tested against.
Monohybrid / dihybrid cross
One gene (F2 expects 3:1) or two genes at once (F2 expects 9:3:3:1) — Mendel's two famous ratios.
Test cross
Crossing against a homozygous recessive to reveal whether a dominant-phenotype individual is AA or Aa.
Incomplete dominance
Heterozygotes look like neither parent (the blue Andalusian chicken): the F2 shows 1:2:1 across three phenotypes.
χ² goodness-of-fit
The test for "do my observed counts match the expected ratio, or is the difference more than chance?" — the lab's verdict-maker.
Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium 1908
The null model of population genetics: with no evolution acting, genotypes settle at p² : 2pq : q². A measured departure is the interesting result.

The kit's own terms

Operation time
The kit's clock: time counted in operations, never wall-clock — so every number reproduces exactly, run after run.
Keys are species
The founding mapping: stored keys are the species list; how often the workload touches a key is its abundance; inserts and removes are births and deaths. Membership is the habitat. Access is the ecology.
.eco protocol
A plain-text experiment file — phases, models, your own data, and hypotheses committed before the run — that the engine runs and grades. See the .eco Reference.
Pre-registration
Writing the hypothesis down before collecting or running the data, so the test can't quietly move after the fact. expect: lines are pre-registration made executable.
FieldReport bands
The fixed, tested thresholds that turn a number into a sentence ("uneven — a few hot keys carry most of the traffic"). Narration and grading share one source of truth.
Oracle test
A test that checks the instrument against a hand-computed answer — why the kit's numbers can be cited.
