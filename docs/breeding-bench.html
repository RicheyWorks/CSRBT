Minimum population

The rule this page applies is seed saved from at least 20 inbreeding or 100 outbreeding individuals per variety — the seed-savers' rule of thumb, not a measured threshold; the per-crop literature refines it in both directions, and here is how far. Seed Savers Exchange’s crop-specific chart asks for 10–20 plants of a self-pollinator, 80 brassicas, and 200 corn, carrots or onions — a lower floor for inbreeders than this page uses and a higher one for outbreeders. One number across all crops is a deliberate simplification rather than an oversight: a floor you remember beats a table you look up, and the crop notes carry the species detail. Both halves matter, and they fail differently.

For an inbreeder — beans, peas, lettuce, tomatoes — the plants are already near-homozygous, so a small sample loses little. Twenty is about avoiding a bottleneck and catching the occasional off-type, not about inbreeding.

For an outbreeder — corn, brassicas, carrots, onions, beets — the plants carry a load of recessive alleles that heterozygosity has been hiding. Sample too few and you force those into the open. Corn, carrots and onions are the classic sufferers, and sweet corn wants at least 100 plants and preferably 200 or more — the population The Seed Garden recommends for long-term stewardship of a corn variety (Colley & Zystro, 2015).

The floor is a floor, not a target. It is the point below which you are actively damaging the variety. If you are trying to conserve diversity rather than merely maintain it, more is better with no sharp upper limit, and the honest advice for a home grower is to grow as many as the space allows.

Effective population size

The number that governs inbreeding is not how many plants you grew but Ne, the effective population size — and unequal contribution pushes it below the headcount. With Nm pollen parents and Nf seed parents contributing equally within each group:

Ne = 4 Nm Nf / (Nm + Nf)

The consequence is unforgiving. Ten pollen parents and ninety seed parents is a hundred plants and an Ne of 36. The rarer sex caps you.

Inbreeding then accumulates each generation at

ΔF = 1 / (2 Ne)

so Ne = 20 adds 2.5% per generation and compounds. Ten generations of that is a different variety, and the decline shows up first as vigour and seed set rather than as the trait you were watching.

These formulas assume an idealised population — random mating, non-overlapping generations, no selection. Real seed saving violates all three, so read Ne as an optimistic upper bound rather than a measurement.

Isolation

Two distances on this page are cited: two miles for sweet corn and half a mile between squash varieties of the same species (NMSU H-262). Everything else is yours to enter, and that is deliberate.

Published isolation tables disagree with each other by an order of magnitude, because the right distance depends on pollinator pressure, wind, intervening barriers, the size of both populations, and — most of all — on what the seed is for. Seed you will sow yourself next year tolerates contamination that certified seed production does not. A page that printed one number for all of that would be confidently wrong most of the time.

Distance is not the only tool. Time isolation — staggering sowings so two varieties do not flower together — works where space does not. Bagging and hand-pollination is absolute where it is practical. A hedge or a building between two plots is worth more than the same distance across open ground.

Note the species traps this page carries in its crop notes, because they cause more surprise crosses than distance ever does: all of cabbage, broccoli, kale, collards, kohlrabi, Brussels sprouts and cauliflower are one species; the three common squash species do not cross with each other but do cross freely within themselves; carrot crosses with the Queen Anne's lace in the verge; and beetroot, chard and sugar beet are one species.

Selection

Saving seed from the best p proportion of a population is truncation selection, and its intensity follows from p alone:

i = φ(x) / p,   where x = Φ-1(1 − p)

Keeping the top 10% gives i = φ(1.2816) / 0.10 ≈ 1.755; the top 50% gives i = φ(0) / 0.50 ≈ 0.798 — that is i = φ(x) ÷ p evaluated at those cutoffs (Falconer & Mackay, 1996), written out with p substituted so you can check both without leaving the page. Then the expected response is

R = i × h² × σP

the same breeder's equation the Selection Log uses on wild populations, and it carries the same warnings. h² is a property of this population in this environment; a field estimate is inflated by the environment parents and offspring share; and the predicted response frequently fails to appear.

Intensity fights population size, and the fight is the whole craft. Selecting the top 5% of 100 plants leaves you five parents. That is a strong selection differential and an Ne around 5, which adds 10% inbreeding in one generation. The gain you make on the trait is paid for in vigour you will notice two generations later. Grow a bigger population, or select less hard.

Roguing — pulling off-types before they shed pollen — is selection with the same arithmetic, applied in the negative. It is also the step most often left out of the record, which is why it has a log here.

Trials

The trial analysis is a randomised complete block design. Blocks absorb the field gradient — the wet corner, the shaded end — so that what is left is the difference between varieties rather than the difference between places.

LSD0.05 = t0.025, df × √(2 × MSE / r)

Two entry means differing by more than the LSD are conventionally called different. CV% = 100√MSE / grand mean measures how noisy the trial was; for vegetable yield trials a CV under roughly 15% is conventionally considered acceptable and above 25% suggests the trial cannot answer the question. Both of those are conventions, not thresholds.

The LSD lies when you have many entries. It is a pairwise test at 5%, so across ten entries there are forty-five comparisons and you should expect two apparent differences from noise alone. Use it to compare each entry against a named control, which is one comparison per entry, and treat "these two middling entries differ" as a hypothesis rather than a result. One site and one season is one site and one season, whatever the LSD says.

A trial with one replicate has no error term and no LSD, because there is nothing to estimate the noise from. The page will compute means and refuse the rest.

Seed

A germination test is a proportion, so it carries a binomial standard error SE = √(p(1−p)/n). Testing 20 seeds and getting 18 up is 90% ± 6.7% — that is a real interval, and it is why 100 seeds is the usual test size where you can spare them.

Storage life: most high-quality, correctly stored seed stays viable at least 3–5 years — a range, not a constant, and one that varies enormously by species. Onion, parsnip and leek are short-lived and worth retesting annually; brassicas, tomatoes and cucurbits often run far longer than five years. The rule of thumb that matters more than any number is that cool and dry beats either alone, and that seed put away damp does not keep however cold it is.

This page does not predict your seed's remaining life from a stored curve, because that depends on your moisture content and your storage temperature. It tracks the test results you actually got, which is the thing that answers the question.
