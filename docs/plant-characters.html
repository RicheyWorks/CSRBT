One

Keying a plant you don't know

Most failed identifications are not failures of knowledge. They are failures of looking — the key asked a question about a part nobody examined.

Work in this order, and write each answer down before moving on. Committing to a character before you know where it leads is the whole discipline; deciding what you saw after you have a candidate in mind is how confirmation bias gets into a dataset.

  1. Habit and habitat first. Woody or herbaceous? Annual, biennial or perennial? Wet or dry, shade or sun, what substrate? Half the flora is eliminated before you touch the plant.
  2. Is it a monocot or a dicot? Parallel veins, parts in threes, no true bark, scattered vascular bundles — versus netted veins and parts in fours or fives. This is the biggest single fork.
  3. Leaf arrangement, at a node you can see. Alternate, opposite, whorled, basal. Check several nodes: arrangement can change up a stem.
  4. Simple or compound — and prove it with the axillary bud, not by eye.
  5. Then the flower, because that is where the keys live: symmetry, part numbers, fusion, ovary position, inflorescence type.
  6. Fruit if you can get it. An astonishing number of couplets need mature fruit. A specimen without it may be unkeyable.
Sterile material is often unidentifiable, and that is not your fault. Many genera — Carex, Salix, Astragalus, most Brassicaceae — simply cannot be keyed to species without flowers or fruit. Record it as Carex sp. and come back when it is fertile. A confident wrong name is far more expensive than an honest genus.

Record what you saw, then what you concluded. They are different columns.

Two

Leaves

The characters you can read without a flower, which is most of the time.

Arrangement

Check at a node partway up the stem — the lowest and uppermost nodes are the least reliable.

AlternateOne leaf per node, staggered up the stem. The commonest state — and the first thing to check, because it splits the flora roughly in half.
OppositeTwo leaves per node, facing. In the western flora think maples, dogwoods, mints, madders — the mnemonic MADCap Horse covers most woody cases.
WhorledThree or more at a node. Uncommon enough that it is nearly diagnostic when you see it.
Basal / rosetteLeaves crowded at ground level, stem leaves reduced or absent. Note whether the flowering stem is leafless (a scape) — that is a real character.

Shape

Shape terms describe the outline. Say where the blade is widest — that is what separates most of the confusable pairs.

OvateEgg-shaped, widest below the middle.
ObovateEgg-shaped but widest above the middle. Say the shape, then check which end is fat.
LanceolateLance-shaped, several times longer than wide, widest below middle.
LinearLong and narrow with parallel sides. Most grasses, many sedges.
CordateHeart-shaped base with a notch at the petiole.
ReniformKidney-shaped — wider than long, notched base.

Margin

EntireSmooth, untoothed. Say it out loud — beginners under-record entire margins.
SerrateSharp teeth pointing forward toward the tip, like a saw.
DentateSharp teeth pointing outward, perpendicular to the margin.
CrenateRounded, scalloped teeth.
LobedIndentations cutting less than halfway to the midrib.
PinnatifidCut more than halfway to the midrib but not to it — still one leaf, not compound.

Simple or compound

The commonest beginner error in the whole discipline is calling leaflets leaves. The test is not size or shape — it is the axillary bud, which sits in the angle where a true leaf meets the stem and never at the base of a leaflet. A whole leaf also abscises as a unit.

SimpleOne blade. The test is the axillary bud: a bud sits where a true leaf meets the stem, never at the base of a leaflet.
Pinnately compoundLeaflets along an axis, feather-fashion. Ash, walnut, most Astragalus.
Bipinnately compoundThe divisions are themselves divided. Many Apiaceae and Fabaceae.
Palmately compoundLeaflets radiating from one point. Lupinus, buckeye.
TrifoliateThree leaflets. Clovers, poison oak, Rubus — worth its own name because it is so common.

Venation

Pinnate venationOne midrib with side veins branching off. Most dicots.
Palmate venationSeveral main veins from the blade base. Maples, currants, many Malvaceae.
Parallel venationVeins running the length of the blade without a dominant midrib. The monocot signature — grasses, sedges, lilies, orchids.
Three

Flowers & inflorescences

Floras key on flowers because flower structure is conservative — it changes slowly and so it tracks relatedness better than leaves, which respond to whatever the site is doing.

How the flowers are arranged

RacemeStalked flowers along an unbranched axis, opening bottom-up. Mustards are the classic.
SpikeLike a raceme but the flowers are stalkless against the axis.
PanicleA branched raceme. Most grasses in flower.
UmbelAll the stalks arising from one point like an umbrella's ribs. Apiaceae is named for it.
Head (capitulum)Many stalkless flowers packed onto a common receptacle and surrounded by bracts, the whole thing masquerading as one flower. Asteraceae.
CymeThe central flower opens first and growth continues from the sides. The opposite developmental order to a raceme.

Symmetry and ovary position

These two characters do more work in keys than almost anything else. Ovary position in particular is worth learning to see: cut a flower in half lengthways with a thumbnail and look at where the sepals and petals attach relative to the ovary.

ActinomorphicRadially symmetric — many planes of symmetry. Rose, lily, buttercup.
ZygomorphicBilaterally symmetric — one plane only. Pea, mint, snapdragon, orchid. Nearly always a pollinator-specialisation signal.
Superior ovaryThe other parts attach below the ovary; the ovary sits free in the flower's cup.
Inferior ovaryThe other parts attach above the ovary, which is fused into the receptacle — look for a swelling below the petals. Apiaceae, Asteraceae, Onagraceae.
The floral formula, in one line. Botanists compress a flower to something like K5 C5 A∞ G(5) — K for calyx (sepals), C for corolla (petals), A for androecium (stamens), G for gynoecium (carpels). A number is the count, ∞ means many, brackets mean fused, and a line under G means an inferior ovary. Writing one forces you to look at every whorl, which is precisely the point.
Four

Grass, sedge or rush

Three families that look alike at forty paces and are not remotely closely related in the way beginners assume. The rhyme is genuinely reliable.

Sedges have edges, rushes are round, grasses have nodes from their tips to the ground.

Grass · PoaceaeSedge · CyperaceaeRush · Juncaceae
Stem sectionRound, usually hollow between nodes Usually triangular, solidRound, pith-filled
NodesSwollen, solid, obvious jointsNot swollenNot swollen
Leaf ranksTwo-ranked (opposite sides)Three-ranked (spiralling)Basal, round or channelled
SheathOpen, split down one sideClosed, a tubeOpen
LigulePresent — and the key characterUsually absentAbsent
FlowerSpikelets, no perianthScales; Carex has a perigynium Six tepals — a real, tiny lily flower
FruitCaryopsis (grain)Achene, three-angled or lens-shapedCapsule, many seeds
The ten-second test. Roll the stem between finger and thumb well below the inflorescence. Sharp corners: sedge. Round and jointed with a papery collar where the blade leaves the stem: grass. Round, smooth, pithy, with a flower that has actual tepals: rush. If it is round and jointed but you feel no ligule, look again — you are probably above the collar.
Five

Work out the family

Answer only what you can actually see. Every answer narrows the list; leaving one blank just keeps it longer. This suggests candidates to check in a flora — it is not a determination.

What this can and cannot do. It matches the characters you enter against the states each family is known to show, so a family stays on the list unless something genuinely rules it out. That makes it forgiving — and it means a top-ranked family is a hypothesis, not an answer. Families are variable: Ranunculaceae will hand you a bilateral flower, some Asteraceae have opposite leaves, Rosaceae switches between superior and inferior ovaries. Take the candidates to a regional flora and key properly. And note the corollary — if nothing scores well, you have probably mis-scored a character rather than found something new.
Six

Twenty families on sight

Experienced botanists do not key from scratch. They recognise a family in a second, which reduces a flora of several thousand species to a few dozen — and then they key. This is that shortcut, for the western North American flora.

Asteraceaesunflower family

Heads of many tiny flowers wearing one flower's costume, wrapped in bracts (the involucre). Ray flowers around the edge, disc flowers in the middle — or only one kind. Inferior ovary; the pappus becomes the parachute.

In the fieldCount the flowers in a 'flower'. If a daisy comes apart into dozens of tubes, you are here. The largest family in most western floras.

Western examplesBalsamorhiza, Artemisia, Ericameria, Achillea, Erigeron

Poaceaegrass family

Round hollow stems with swollen, closed nodes. Leaves two-ranked with a split sheath and a ligule where blade meets sheath. Flowers reduced to spikelets with bracts, no perianth.

In the fieldRoll the stem: grasses are round and the nodes are solid and jointed. Then look for the ligule — its shape and size key more grasses than anything else.

Western examplesPseudoroegneria spicata, Bromus tectorum, Achnatherum, Calamagrostis

Cyperaceaesedge family

Stems usually solid and triangular in section, leaves three-ranked, sheaths closed. Achenes; Carex has the perigynium, a sac around the achene.

In the fieldSedges have edges — roll the stem between finger and thumb and feel the three corners. Then check for perigynia, which settle Carex.

Western examplesCarex, Eleocharis, Schoenoplectus

Juncaceaerush family

Stems round and usually pith-filled; leaves round or channelled. Flowers small but genuinely lily-like — six tepals, three or six stamens, a capsule.

In the fieldRushes are round. The giveaway is that the flower, under a lens, has real tepals — grasses and sedges do not.

Western examplesJuncus, Luzula

Fabaceaepea family

Zygomorphic flower of banner, two wings, and a fused keel; fruit a legume splitting along two sutures. Leaves usually compound with stipules. Most fix nitrogen via root nodules.

In the fieldThe pea flower is unmistakable once seen. Failing flowers, look for compound leaves plus a pod — and dig a root to look for nodules.

Western examplesLupinus, Astragalus, Trifolium, Cytisus scoparius (invasive)

Brassicaceaemustard family

Four petals in a cross, six stamens with four long and two short (tetradynamous), fruit a silique or silicle splitting to leave a papery partition. Peppery smell and taste.

In the fieldFour petals, a cross, and a mustard smell when you crush a leaf. Then look at fruit shape — long silique or short silicle — because that is where the keys go.

Western examplesArabis, Draba, Descurainia, Lepidium

Rosaceaerose family

Five petals, many stamens, often five sepals plus an epicalyx; a hypanthium is usually present. Stipules on the leaves. Fruit type varies enormously — pome, drupe, achene, aggregate.

In the fieldFive petals with a boss of many stamens, plus stipules. The family is held together by flower structure, not by fruit.

Western examplesPurshia, Cercocarpus, Amelanchier, Rubus, Potentilla

Apiaceaecarrot family

Compound umbels, hollow ribbed stems, sheathing petiole bases, leaves usually much divided. Tiny five-petalled flowers with an inferior ovary; fruit a schizocarp splitting into two.

In the fieldAn umbel over a sheathing leaf base. Never taste to identify — this family holds both wild carrot and water hemlock, the most toxic plant in North America.

Western examplesLomatium, Heracleum, Perideridia, Cicuta (deadly)

Lamiaceaemint family

Square stems, opposite leaves, aromatic. Two-lipped zygomorphic corolla, four nutlets in the fruit.

In the fieldRoll the stem and smell your fingers. Square plus opposite plus a smell is the family in three seconds — but square stems alone are not enough.

Western examplesMonardella, Prunella, Mentha, Salvia

Ranunculaceaebuttercup family

Parts usually many and free — many stamens, many pistils. Sepals often petal-like. Leaves commonly divided, often with a sheathing base. Considered an 'primitive' floral arrangement.

In the fieldEverything numerous and nothing fused. If the flower looks like a scatter of separate parts, start here.

Western examplesRanunculus, Delphinium, Aquilegia, Clematis, Anemone

Ericaceaeheath family

Woody, usually evergreen, thick leathery leaves. Urn- or bell-shaped fused corolla; anthers opening by pores, often with awns. Almost always on acid soils, and heavily dependent on ericoid or arbutoid mycorrhizae.

In the fieldAn urn-shaped flower on a leathery evergreen shrub. In the PNW that is salal, huckleberry, manzanita, kinnikinnick.

Western examplesGaultheria shallon, Vaccinium, Arctostaphylos

Polygonaceaebuckwheat family

Swollen nodes; many genera have an ocrea, a papery sheath wrapping the stem at the node. Perianth of tepals, not differentiated into sepals and petals; fruit a three-angled achene.

In the fieldLook at the node for a papery collar. Eriogonum lacks it but is unmistakable in the Great Basin — dense involucres of tiny flowers on a wiry shrub.

Western examplesEriogonum, Rumex, Polygonum

Amaranthaceaegoosefoot family

Includes the old Chenopodiaceae. Small greenish flowers without petals; leaves often mealy, fleshy, or scurfy; many are halophytes with C4 photosynthesis.

In the fieldGrey, mealy, saline-flat shrubs with unshowy flowers. In the Great Basin this family is the valley bottom.

Western examplesAtriplex, Sarcobatus, Krascheninnikovia, Salsola (invasive)

Boraginaceaeborage family

Coiled inflorescence (a scorpioid cyme) unrolling as it flowers; rough bristly hairs; four nutlets whose sculpturing is the main key character.

In the fieldThe fiddlehead-shaped flowering coil plus sandpaper texture. Then collect ripe nutlets, because the keys need them.

Western examplesCryptantha, Mertensia, Amsinckia, Hackelia

Plantaginaceaeplantain family

Much broader than it used to be — now holds most former Scrophulariaceae including the penstemons. Zygomorphic tubular corolla, usually four or five lobes, ovary superior, fruit a capsule.

In the fieldIn the West: a tubular two-lipped flower on a herb, with a superior ovary and a capsule, is usually here. Penstemon has the sterile fifth stamen — the 'beard tongue'.

Western examplesPenstemon, Castilleja (in Orobanchaceae now), Veronica, Plantago

Onagraceaeevening primrose family

Flower parts usually in fours, with a long hypanthium above an obviously inferior ovary — the flower sits on a long slender 'stalk' that is actually the ovary.

In the fieldCount to four, then look for the swollen inferior ovary below a long floral tube.

Western examplesChamerion angustifolium (fireweed), Oenothera, Epilobium, Clarkia

Caryophyllaceaepink family

Opposite leaves with swollen nodes, stems often jointed like a firecracker; petals frequently notched or fringed; free-central placentation in the capsule.

In the fieldOpposite leaves and conspicuously swollen joints. Petals that look snipped with scissors seal it.

Western examplesSilene, Cerastium, Stellaria, Arenaria

Salicaceaewillow family

Dioecious catkins, no showy perianth, seeds with cottony hairs. Salix has one bud scale like a hood; Populus has several.

In the fieldOne cap-like bud scale means willow. Riparian, and the genus is notoriously hard — collect both catkins and mature leaves or do not bother keying to species.

Western examplesSalix, Populus

Saxifragaceaesaxifrage family

Often basal rosettes on rock or moist ledges, five petals, a partly inferior ovary with two beaked carpels that look like little horns.

In the fieldTwo horns on the ripening fruit, on a rosette plant from a wet cliff.

Western examplesSaxifraga, Heuchera, Tiarella, Micranthes

Liliaceae s.l.lily group family

Now split into many families (Melanthiaceae, Themidaceae, Asparagaceae…), but in the field: monocot, parts in threes, parallel veins, showy tepals.

In the fieldThrees and parallel veins with a showy flower. Then accept that the family name has probably changed since your flora was printed, and record the genus.

Western examplesTrillium, Calochortus, Erythronium, Maianthemum

Family limits move. Molecular phylogenetics has redrawn a great deal since the floras on most shelves were printed: Scrophulariaceae was dismembered (penstemons to Plantaginaceae, paintbrushes to Orobanchaceae), Chenopodiaceae folded into Amaranthaceae, Liliaceae split many ways. Record the genus and species, note which flora and edition you keyed with, and let the family follow whatever your regional authority currently says.
Seven

Making a specimen worth keeping

A voucher is what turns your observation into a claim someone else can check in fifty years. An unvouchered record is a memory.

What makes it valid

  • Fertile material. Flowers, fruit, or both. Without them the specimen may be unkeyable and therefore unverifiable — which is the whole purpose gone.
  • Enough of the plant. For herbs, the whole plant including roots or the rhizome base — underground parts carry real characters. For woody plants, a shoot showing leaf arrangement, a node, and reproductive parts.
  • More than one, where the population can spare it. Duplicates let you send material to a specialist and keep a copy.
  • Notes taken alive. Flower colour, scent, exudate, height, habit — everything that dies in the press. This is the part nobody can reconstruct later.

What goes on the label

The label is the specimen. A perfectly pressed plant with a bad label is decoration.

  • Scientific name, and who determined it (det.) and when
  • Country, state, county, and a locality description precise enough to relocate
  • Latitude and longitude with the datum, plus a coordinate uncertainty
  • Elevation, habitat, associated species, aspect and slope
  • Description of the living plant — colour, height, abundance, phenology
  • Collector name and collection number, and the date

The Stand Sheet and the Relevé both generate this block from what you have already entered, so the label is a by-product of the plot rather than a second job at the end of the day.

Pressing, briefly

  • Arrange before you close the press. Show one leaf underside. Fold long stems in a V or N rather than cutting them.
  • Thick fruits, bulbs and cones: slice in half and press both faces.
  • Change the newsprint daily for the first few days. Wet specimens mould, and a mouldy sheet can spread through a cabinet.
  • Succulents and orchids need killing first (a brief alcohol dip) or they keep growing in the press.
Before you collect anything. Get permission — most public land requires a permit, and collecting is prohibited outright in many reserves and all national parks without one. Never collect a rare, listed, or locally uncommon plant; photograph it and record the population instead. The usual field ethic is to take no more than one in twenty individuals present, and none at all from a population under about fifty plants. For anything you suspect is rare, the record belongs to your state Natural Heritage Program, not to your press.
